1 What is it
- SRE is a way of running production systems using software engineering. You set measurable reliability targets and let those targets drive your decisions on releases, alerts and incident handling.
- SLI (service level indicator) is a measured number, such as the share of requests that succeed.
- SLO (service level objective) is the internal target for an SLI, such as 99.9 percent of requests succeed over 30 days.
- SLA (service level agreement) is the contract with a client, with penalties if missed. The SLO should be stricter than the SLA.
- Error budget is the allowed unreliability, which is 100 percent minus the SLO.
- Legacy equivalent: the client SLA clause plus the "support team keeps the servers up" model, where reliability was a feeling and uptime was a monthly report.

------------------------------------------------------------

2 Why needed
- Without targets, dev wants to ship fast and ops wants to change nothing, and nobody can settle the argument.
- "100 percent uptime" is impossible and very costly, so teams overspend on reliability or underspend by accident.
- Alerts based on guesses cause fatigue. Postmortems based on blame hide the real causes.
- SRE gives one shared number that both sides accept.

------------------------------------------------------------

3 Before you read
- Request, latency, error rate: the basic signals a service produces.
- Percentile: p99 latency means 99 percent of requests were faster than this value.
- Monitoring: metrics and logs are already being collected (see 13.01).
- Percent math: 99.9 percent leaves 0.1 percent for failure.

4 Features and core concepts
- SLI: a ratio of good events to valid events. Why it matters: it must reflect what the user feels, not what the server feels. Typical SLIs are availability, latency and correctness.
- SLO: a target on an SLI over a window, such as 30 days rolling. Why it matters: it turns "reliable enough" into a number you can defend.
- SLA: the external promise with consequences. Why it matters: it sets the floor, and your SLO sits above it with a safety margin.
- Error budget: 100 percent minus SLO. For 99.9 percent over 30 days the budget is about 43 minutes of full outage. Why it matters: it is the amount of risk you are allowed to spend.
- Toil: manual, repetitive, automatable work that grows with the service and has no lasting value. Why it matters: SRE teams cap toil, often at about half of their time (verify), and automate the rest.
- Release gating by budget: if the budget is spent, freeze risky releases and spend effort on reliability. If budget remains, ship faster. Why it matters: it replaces opinion with data.
- Availability nines table: 99 percent is about 3.65 days of downtime a year. 99.9 percent is about 8.8 hours. 99.99 percent is about 53 minutes. 99.999 percent is about 5 minutes. Why it matters: each extra nine costs far more than the last.
- Incident management: roles (incident commander, communications lead, operations lead, scribe), severity levels, communication cadence, and a timeline kept during the incident. Why it matters: roles prevent ten people debugging the same thing while nobody updates the client.
- Blameless postmortem: a written review with timeline, impact, root and contributing causes, what went well, and action items with owners and dates. Why it matters: people report problems openly when they are not punished.
- On-call practices: primary and secondary rotation, handover notes, runbooks linked from alerts, fair load, and paying or compensating for out-of-hours work. Why it matters: burnout ends careers and causes more incidents.
- Change management: lightweight review, small changes, rollback plan, change calendar for risky windows. Why it matters: most outages follow a change.
- Production readiness review (PRR): a checklist before a service goes live, covering SLOs, alerts, runbooks, capacity, rollback, security and on-call ownership. Why it matters: it catches gaps before users do.

Worth memorizing:
- Availability SLI = good requests / valid requests
- Error budget = 1 - SLO

5 Core focus (exam and interview)
- Know the difference between SLI, SLO and SLA, and which one carries a penalty (SLA).
- Be able to compute the budget from an SLO, and the downtime from a number of nines.
- Know the rule: SLO stricter than SLA.
- Know that error budget policy decides what happens when budget runs out (feature freeze, reliability work).
- Composite SLA: serial dependencies multiply. Two services at 99.9 percent each give about 99.8 percent together.
- Azure link for AZ-305 and AZ-400: Azure SLAs are per service, composite SLA math is asked in design questions, and Azure Monitor and Application Insights supply the SLIs.
- Postmortems are blameless by definition. "Root cause: human error" is not an acceptable ending.
- Limits and numbers on service SLAs change, so check the current Azure SLA page (verify).

6 Real-world use case
- Situation: an Azure App Service API backed by Azure SQL serves a client portal. Releases happen weekly and every release triggers a "was it us?" argument with the client.
- Choice made: define two SLIs from Application Insights, request success rate and p95 latency. Set SLO at 99.9 percent success and p95 under 500 ms over 30 days. The client SLA stays at 99.5 percent, giving margin.
- Error budget policy: if more than 50 percent of the budget is gone in the first half of the window, only bug fixes and reliability work go out. Alerts fire on budget burn rate, not on single spikes.
- Result: release decisions become a dashboard check, and postmortems feed fixes into the backlog.
- Trade-off: measuring and reviewing takes effort, and a strict SLO slows feature delivery.
- When NOT to use it: an internal tool with five users, a prototype, or a service nobody depends on. Use simple uptime checks there. Also do not set an SLO you cannot measure.

7 Interview lens
- Q: What is the difference between SLI, SLO and SLA?
  A: SLI is the measurement, SLO is the internal target on it, SLA is the contract with penalties. SLO is set stricter than SLA.
- Q: What is an error budget and how do you use it?
  A: It is 100 percent minus the SLO, the amount of failure you can afford. When it is spent, risky releases stop and reliability work takes priority.
- Q: How do you run an incident?
  A: Declare severity, assign an incident commander and communications lead, mitigate first and diagnose after, keep a timeline, update stakeholders on a fixed cadence. Then write a blameless postmortem.
- Q: Why not aim for 100 percent availability?
  A: It is not achievable, each extra nine costs much more, and users cannot tell the difference beyond their own network's reliability. The budget lets you trade reliability against delivery speed.

8 Common mistakes
- SLI measured at the server, not the user: the dashboard shows green while users see errors. Fix: measure at the load balancer or from synthetic checks as well.
- SLO equal to SLA: any small miss becomes a contract breach. Fix: keep an internal margin.
- Too many SLOs: nobody tracks them. Fix: two to four per service.
- Budget policy written but never enforced: releases continue after the budget is gone. Fix: get management agreement in advance.
- Postmortem that blames a person: people hide mistakes next time. Fix: ask what in the system allowed the error.
- Postmortem actions with no owner or date: the same incident returns. Fix: track actions like backlog items.
- On-call with no runbooks: every page needs the one expert. Fix: link a runbook in each alert.
- Counting toil as normal work: the team never gets time to automate. Fix: measure it and cap it.

9 Mental model
- Think of a fuel tank for risk.

  SLO 99.9 percent
       |
  Error budget = fuel tank (about 43 minutes per 30 days)
       |
  Releases, incidents and experiments burn fuel
       |
  Tank has fuel: ship faster
  Tank empty: stop, fix reliability, refill next window

10 Mini lab
Time: about 30 minutes. Tools: a calculator or spreadsheet, plus a text editor. No Azure needed.
- Step 1: pick one service you know well. Write one availability SLI as good requests over valid requests.
- Step 2: set an SLO of 99.9 percent over 30 days. Compute the budget: 30 days x 24 x 60 = 43,200 minutes, times 0.001 = 43.2 minutes.
- Step 3: write a three-line error budget policy: what happens at 50 percent used, at 100 percent used, and who approves exceptions.
- Step 4: write a blameless postmortem for any past incident you remember, using headings: summary, impact, timeline, causes, what went well, actions with owner and date.
- Step 5: compute the composite SLA of two serial services at 99.95 percent each (0.9995 x 0.9995, about 99.90 percent).
- Expected result: one page with SLI, SLO, budget of about 43 minutes, policy, one postmortem, and the composite figure.
- Clean-up: none. Keep the page as raw material for 17.01 story bank.
