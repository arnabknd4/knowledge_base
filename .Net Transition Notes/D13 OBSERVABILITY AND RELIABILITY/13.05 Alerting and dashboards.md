1 What is it

- Alerting is the practice of automatically notifying a person or system when a measured signal says users are hurt or about to be. A dashboard is a screen that shows system health at a glance and helps you find the cause.
- An alert rule has a condition (for example error rate above 2 percent for 5 minutes), a severity and a receiver (email, SMS, Teams, pager).
- Symptom alert: user-visible pain (slow pages, failed requests). Cause alert: an internal reason (high CPU, full disk).
- Escalation means: if the first person does not respond, the alert goes to the next person or a manager.
- Paging policy means: the rules for which alerts wake someone at night and which wait for morning.
- Runbook means: a short written guide of steps to take when a specific alert fires.
- Legacy equivalent: Windows Performance Monitor thresholds, SQL Server Agent alerts and a shared ops mailbox that got filled with emails nobody read.

2 Why needed

- Without alerts, users find outages before you do.
- Too many alerts (alert fatigue) make people ignore all of them, including the real one.
- Without runbooks, every incident starts from zero and depends on one person who remembers.
- Without dashboards, you open ten tools in an incident and lose time.
- Without SLO-based alerting, you either page for harmless blips or miss slow, steady failure.

3 Before you read

- SLI is a measurement of user experience (success rate, latency). SLO is the target for that measurement (99.9 percent success over 30 days).
- Metric, log and trace: numbers over time, event records, and the path of one request across services.
- Azure Monitor and Application Insights collect these signals (topic 13.03).
- Error budget: the amount of failure the SLO allows (0.1 percent for a 99.9 percent SLO).

4 Features and core concepts

- Symptom vs cause alerts
  - Meaning: alert on what users feel, use cause metrics only for diagnosis.
  - Why it matters: high CPU with healthy users is not an emergency; a failing checkout is, even with CPU normal.
- Alert fatigue
  - Meaning: people stop reacting because alerts are too many, too noisy or not actionable.
  - Why it matters: it is the main reason real incidents are missed.
  - Test for every alert: does it need a human now, and does the human know what to do?
- Severity
  - Meaning: a level showing how bad and how urgent (Sev0 or Sev1 critical, Sev2 high, Sev3 low, Sev4 info).
  - Why it matters: it decides who is called and how fast. Azure Monitor alerts have Sev 0 to 4.
- Runbooks
  - Meaning: step-by-step response notes linked from the alert: what it means, how to check, how to fix, who to call.
  - Why it matters: shortens recovery time and lets a new person act safely.
- Escalation
  - Meaning: a timed chain: primary on-call, then secondary, then team lead, then manager.
  - Why it matters: a missed alert must not become a missed incident.
- Dashboard design (overview and drill-down)
  - Meaning: an overview dashboard shows 5 to 8 top signals (traffic, errors, latency, saturation, SLO status). Drill-down dashboards show one service or one dependency in detail.
  - Why it matters: during an incident, you go from "something is wrong" to "where" in under a minute.
- SLO burn-rate alerts
  - Meaning: burn rate is how fast you are spending the error budget. Burn rate 1 means the budget lasts exactly the SLO window. Burn rate 14 means it is gone in about 2 days of a 30-day window.
  - Why it matters: it pages only when the budget is really at risk. Common pattern: a fast-burn alert (high burn over a short window, pages now) plus a slow-burn alert (low burn over a long window, creates a ticket). Use two windows (long and short) to avoid false alarms and to stop quickly after recovery. Exact thresholds (verify) against the Google SRE workbook.
- Synthetic monitoring
  - Meaning: a scripted probe that calls your app from outside on a schedule (login, search, checkout).
  - Why it matters: finds outages at 3 AM when no real user is online. In Azure: Application Insights availability tests (verify current test types).
- Paging policy
  - Meaning: the written rule for what pages a person, what goes to chat or ticket, and who is on call when.
  - Why it matters: protects sleep and focus; only urgent, actionable, user-impacting alerts page.
- Memorize (Azure Monitor alert pieces):
  - alert rule = scope + condition + action group
  - action group = notification (email, SMS, push, voice) + automation (webhook, Function, Logic App, runbook)
  - alert states: New, Acknowledged, Closed; monitor condition: Fired, Resolved

5 Core focus (exam and interview)

- Asked most: symptom vs cause, how to reduce alert noise, what a good alert contains, burn-rate idea, who gets paged.
- A good alert: actionable, urgent, user-related, has an owner, links to a runbook and a dashboard.
- Azure Monitor alert types: metric alerts, log search alerts (KQL), activity log alerts, smart detection in Application Insights. Check the current list on Microsoft Learn (verify).
- Alert processing rules can suppress alerts during planned maintenance. Use them instead of deleting alerts.
- Action groups are reusable: one group can serve many rules.
- Dashboard rules: top row for SLO status, then the four golden signals (latency, traffic, errors, saturation), then dependencies.
- Common numbers to remember: 99.9 percent over 30 days is about 43 minutes of budget; 99.99 percent is about 4.3 minutes.

6 Real-world use case

- Situation: a .NET 8 order API on Azure App Service with Azure SQL. The team had 60 alerts, mostly CPU and memory warnings sent to a group mailbox. A payment failure ran 40 minutes before anyone noticed.
- Choice:
  - SLO: 99.5 percent of checkout requests succeed under 2 seconds.
  - Two paging alerts only: fast burn on checkout SLO (Sev1, pages primary on-call) and a synthetic checkout test failing from two regions.
  - Cause metrics (CPU, DTU, queue length) moved to a drill-down dashboard and to ticket-only alerts.
  - Each paging alert links to a runbook and the overview workbook.
  - Escalation: primary, then secondary after 10 minutes, then team lead after 20 minutes.
- Trade-off: fewer alerts means you might miss a slow cause until it hurts users, so slow-burn tickets and weekly review cover that gap.
- When NOT to use: a tiny internal tool with 10 users does not need burn-rate alerts and on-call rotas. A simple availability check and an email is enough.

7 Interview lens

- Q: How do you reduce alert fatigue?
  - A: Alert on symptoms tied to SLOs and page only for urgent, actionable items. Move cause signals to dashboards and tickets, and review noisy alerts every week.
- Q: What is a burn-rate alert?
  - A: It fires when the error budget is being spent faster than planned. Fast burn over a short window pages; slow burn over a long window opens a ticket.
- Q: What must an alert contain?
  - A: Clear title, severity, affected service, owner, link to dashboard and runbook, and a first action. If nobody can act on it, delete or downgrade it.
- Q: How do you design an incident dashboard?
  - A: Overview with SLO status and golden signals, then drill-down per service and dependency. Keep it to what answers "is it broken, where, since when".

8 Common mistakes

- Alerting on every CPU spike: pages at night for harmless load. Fix: alert on user symptoms, keep CPU as a ticket or dashboard item.
- No runbook link: the on-call person wastes 15 minutes finding what to do. Fix: make runbook URL a required field in the alert template.
- One giant dashboard with 40 charts: nobody reads it in an incident. Fix: overview plus drill-down.
- Static thresholds on traffic that changes by hour: false alarms at peak, misses at night. Fix: use SLO ratios, dynamic thresholds (verify availability) or compare to the same time last week.
- Alerts with no owner: everyone assumes someone else handles it. Fix: tag each alert with a team and route to that team's action group.
- No review: noise grows. Fix: monthly alert review; delete alerts that never led to action.

9 Mental model

Think of a smoke alarm system in a building.

- Smoke detector in a room = cause alert (useful, but only a clue).
- Alarm at the fire station = symptom or SLO alert (people are in danger, act now).
- Wall map with a button = runbook.
- Phone tree = escalation.
- Quiet-hours rule = paging policy.
- Fire drill = synthetic monitoring.
- Control room screens = dashboards.

Users hurt? -> page. Only a clue? -> ticket or dashboard.

10 Mini lab (30 to 40 minutes, Azure free tier)

Goal: one alert with a runbook link and a small overview workbook.

Steps:
- Create a resource group and a Log Analytics workspace plus a workspace-based Application Insights resource (free tier ingestion allowance, verify current limits).
- Deploy any small web app to App Service (free F1 plan) with Application Insights attached. If you have none, create the default sample app.
- In Application Insights, open Availability and add a Standard test (URL ping) to your app URL, run every 5 minutes from 2 locations.
- Create an action group with your email as the only receiver.
- Create an alert rule on the availability test failing (or on Failed requests above 0 for 5 minutes). Set severity to 2. In the alert description paste a fake runbook line: "1 Open the app URL. 2 Check App Service restart. 3 Escalate to team lead."
- Create a workbook with 4 tiles: availability percentage, failed requests, server response time, and requests count.
- Test: stop the App Service, wait for the alert email, check the alert in Azure Monitor Alerts, then start the app and confirm the alert resolves.
- Expected result: you receive a fired email, the alert shows state New with condition Fired, and later condition Resolved.
- Clean-up: delete the resource group, which removes the app, Application Insights, workspace, alert rule and action group.

11 Revision summary

- Page on symptoms, use causes for diagnosis.
- An alert must be urgent, actionable and owned, or it should not page.
- Alert rule = scope + condition + action group.
- Burn rate 1 spends the budget exactly over the SLO window; fast burn pages, slow burn tickets.
- 99.9 percent over 30 days is about 43 minutes of error budget.
- Every paging alert links to a runbook and a dashboard.
- Dashboards: overview first, drill-down second.
- Synthetic tests find outages when no real users are online.
- Paging policy and escalation protect both users and the team.
