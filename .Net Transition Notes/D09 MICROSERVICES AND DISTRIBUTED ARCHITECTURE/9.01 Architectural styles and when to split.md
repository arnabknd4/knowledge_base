1 What is it
- A topic about choosing how to structure a system: monolith, modular monolith, SOA, microservices or serverless. The question is where to draw the lines and what each choice costs.
- Monolith: one deployable unit. Modular monolith: one deployable unit with strict internal module boundaries. SOA (service-oriented architecture): a few large services shared across a company, often through a central bus. Microservices: many small services, each deployed on its own. Serverless: code that runs on demand with no server for you to manage.
- Legacy equivalent: your IIS-hosted MVC solution with several class library projects, plus WCF services shared between applications.

2 Why needed
- Teams split systems for fashion, not need. This topic gives you the real reasons to split and the real costs.
- Without it, you build a distributed monolith: services that must be deployed together and fail together. You pay network cost and get no independence.
- It is the first question in every architect interview: "should we move to microservices?"

3 Before you read
- Deployment unit: the thing you ship and restart as one piece.
- Coupling: how much one part must change when another changes.
- Network call vs method call: a network call can be slow, can fail, and can time out.

4 Features and core concepts
- Monolith: one codebase, one process, one deployment. Why it matters: simplest to build, test, debug and run. It is the right default for small teams.
- Modular monolith: one deployment, but modules have clear boundaries and own their data tables. Why it matters: you keep simple operations and can split a module out later.
- SOA: coarse services shared across business units, often with a central integration layer such as an enterprise service bus. Why it matters: it is the historical step before microservices, and many Indian enterprise clients still run it.
- Microservices: small services, each independently deployable, each owning its data. Why it matters: independent release and scaling per team.
- Serverless: functions or managed services billed per use, scaled by the platform (Azure Functions, Container Apps jobs). Why it matters: low operations load for spiky or event-driven work.
- Benefits and costs: microservices buy independent deployment, scaling and fault isolation. They cost network failures, data consistency problems, harder debugging, and heavier operations.
- Team size and independent deployment as the real drivers: split only when teams block each other on releases or when parts need different scaling. Why it matters: "it is modern" is not a reason.
- Conway's law: system structure copies the communication structure of the organisation. Why it matters: if teams are not separated, services will not stay separated. Design teams and services together.
- Signs of a distributed monolith: services release together, share one database, or one change forces edits in many services. Why it matters: you have the cost of microservices with no benefit.
- Fallacies of distributed computing: false beliefs such as "the network is reliable", "latency is zero", "bandwidth is infinite", "the network is secure", "topology does not change", "there is one administrator", "transport cost is zero", "the network is homogeneous". Why it matters: each one is a bug waiting in production.
- Latency and consistency costs: each hop adds delay, and data in many databases cannot be updated in one transaction. Why it matters: you must accept eventual consistency or design around it.
- Worth memorizing: availability of a synchronous chain is the product of each service's availability. Five services at 99.9 percent each give about 99.5 percent.

5 Core focus (exam and interview)
- Most asked: when to choose a monolith over microservices, and what a distributed monolith is.
- Expected answer shape: name the driver (team size, release independence, scaling need), then the cost, then a middle option (modular monolith).
- Remember: microservices are an organisational pattern as much as a technical one.
- Remember: modular monolith is the safe default start for a new product with unclear boundaries.
- Azure exam angle: architecture styles in the Azure Architecture Center (N-tier, web-queue-worker, microservices, event-driven). Match the style to the workload (verify current names).
- Serverless limits to recall: cold starts, execution time limits, vendor-specific triggers (verify per plan).

6 Real-world use case
- Situation: a 12-year-old MVC monolith on IIS, 25 developers in three teams, releases every six weeks because every change needs full regression.
- Choice: do not rewrite to microservices. First turn it into a modular monolith with module boundaries and separate schemas. Then extract only the module that needs independent scaling or a faster release cycle, using YARP in front (see 9.03).
- Trade-off: slower visible progress, but low risk, and each extraction is reversible.
- When NOT to use microservices: fewer than about three teams, unclear domain boundaries, no CI/CD or monitoring maturity, or no one to run on-call.
- When NOT to use serverless: steady heavy load with predictable cost, or long-running jobs.

7 Interview lens
- Q: When would you choose a modular monolith over microservices?
  A: When the team is small and the domain boundaries are still uncertain. You get clean modules and simple deployment, and you can split later along proven boundaries.
- Q: How do you spot a distributed monolith?
  A: Services must be released together, share a database, or one change ripples through many services. The fix is to merge them or to restore real boundaries and data ownership.
- Q: What does Conway's law mean for architecture?
  A: Your system will mirror how your teams communicate. If you want independent services, first create independent teams with clear ownership.
- Q: What is the real cost of a network call versus a method call?
  A: It can be slow, fail, time out or arrive twice. You must add timeouts, retries, idempotency and tracing, and each hop lowers combined availability.

8 Common mistakes
- Splitting by technical layer (a "database service", a "validation service"). Shows up as every feature touching every service. Fix: split by business capability.
- Sharing one database across services. Shows up as schema changes breaking other teams. Fix: one owner per data set, expose data through APIs or events.
- Too many tiny services too early. Shows up as chatty calls and high latency. Fix: start larger, split when pain is measured.
- Ignoring operations cost. Shows up as no tracing, no alerts, slow incident response. Fix: require CI/CD, monitoring and ownership before extraction.
- Treating the network as reliable. Shows up as cascading timeouts. Fix: timeouts, retries with backoff, circuit breakers (see 3.04).

9 Mental model
- A dial from "one building" to "a city of shops".

  monolith -> modular monolith -> SOA -> microservices -> serverless
  simple ops, tight coupling ----------> loose coupling, heavy ops

- Move right only when team or scaling pain justifies the extra operations cost.

10 Mini lab
- Goal: feel the cost of a network hop and see availability math. Time: about 30 minutes. Tools: .NET SDK, local only.
- Step 1: create a minimal API project with two endpoints in one app: /orders/{id} that calls a local method GetCustomer(id).
- Step 2: time 1000 calls and note the average. This is your method-call baseline.
- Step 3: create a second minimal API project "customers" returning a customer. Change the first app to call it with HttpClient.
- Step 4: time 1000 calls again. Expect a clear increase in latency.
- Step 5: stop the customers app and call /orders/1. Expect an error or timeout. Note that the orders feature is now down.
- Step 6: compute on paper: three services at 99.9 percent in a chain give 0.999 x 0.999 x 0.999, about 99.7 percent.
- Result you should see: slower calls and a new failure mode that did not exist in the monolith.
- Clean-up: delete both project folders.

11 Revision summary
- Monolith, modular monolith, SOA, microservices, serverless sit on one dial of independence versus operations cost.
- The real drivers for splitting are team independence, release independence and different scaling needs.
- Conway's law: architecture mirrors team communication.
- Distributed monolith: services that deploy, fail or share data together.
- Synchronous chain availability is the product of each service's availability.
- The fallacies of distributed computing begin with "the network is reliable".
- Modular monolith is the safe default start; extract services later along proven boundaries.
- Do not split before CI/CD, monitoring and ownership exist.
