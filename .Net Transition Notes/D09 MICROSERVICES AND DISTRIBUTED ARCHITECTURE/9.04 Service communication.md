1 What is it
- Service communication is how the parts of a distributed system talk to each other. You pick a style (REST, gRPC or messaging) and decide how calls are found, balanced, retried and versioned.
- Synchronous means the caller waits for the answer. Asynchronous means the caller sends a message and moves on.
- BFF (Backend for Frontend) is a small API layer built for one kind of client, such as the Angular web app.
- Legacy equivalent: WCF service references and SOAP calls between tiers, with web service URLs hard-coded in Web.config.

2 Why needed
- Once one app becomes many services, every call is a network call. Networks are slow and fail at random.
- Without clear rules, one slow service stalls all its callers and a small outage spreads across the system.
- Without versioning, changing one service breaks every consumer on the next deploy.

4 Features and core concepts
- REST vs gRPC vs messaging
  - REST is JSON over HTTP. It is simple, readable and works with every client, but it is text-heavy and loosely typed.
  - gRPC is a binary protocol over HTTP/2 with a typed contract (protobuf). It is fast and supports streaming, but browsers need gRPC-Web.
  - Messaging means sending a message to a broker (Service Bus) instead of calling the service directly. The caller does not wait and the receiver can be down for a while.
  - Why it matters: choose by need, not fashion. Public and browser-facing is REST. Internal and high-volume is gRPC. Work that can wait is messaging.
- Availability math of synchronous call chains
  - If A calls B calls C in a chain, total availability is the product of each one.
  - Three services at 99.9 percent each give about 99.7 percent overall (0.999 x 0.999 x 0.999).
  - Why it matters: every extra synchronous hop lowers availability and adds latency. This is the main argument for async.
- Timeouts and retries
  - A timeout is the longest you will wait for a reply. A retry sends the call again after a failure.
  - Why it matters: no timeout means threads pile up. Retrying without a limit causes retry storms. Retry only idempotent calls (calls safe to repeat).
- Service discovery
  - It is how a caller finds the current address of a service, since instances come and go.
  - In AKS it is cluster DNS (service name). In Container Apps it is the app name inside the environment. In App Service it is a fixed URL.
  - Why it matters: hard-coded IPs break at the first scale-out or restart.
- Load balancing
  - It spreads calls across instances. L4 works on TCP. L7 works on HTTP and can route by path or header.
  - Why it matters: gRPC reuses one long HTTP/2 connection, so a plain L4 balancer sends all calls to one pod. Use L7 or client-side balancing.
- API versioning
  - Run old and new contract versions together, in the URL (/v2/orders) or a header.
  - Why it matters: consumers upgrade at their own pace.
- BFF
  - One backend per client type (web, mobile) that shapes data for that client.
  - Why it matters: the Angular app makes one call instead of five, and web and mobile do not fight over one API shape.
- Gateway aggregation
  - The gateway (API Management or YARP) calls several services and returns one combined response.
  - Why it matters: it reduces chatty client calls. The risk is that the gateway becomes a mini-monolith with business logic.
- Async request-reply
  - The client sends a request, gets 202 Accepted with a status URL, and polls or gets a callback. Or it uses a reply queue with a correlation ID.
  - Why it matters: long work does not hold an HTTP connection open.
- Orchestration vs choreography
  - Orchestration: one coordinator tells each service what to do next. Choreography: each service reacts to events with no central boss.
  - Why it matters: orchestration is easier to follow and debug. Choreography is looser but harder to trace.
- Backward compatibility
  - A new version of a service must still work for old consumers. Add fields, do not remove or rename them. Consumers must ignore unknown fields.
  - Why it matters: it allows independent deployment, the whole point of microservices.
- Worth memorizing: an HttpClient with a 5 second timeout and a retry policy via AddStandardResilienceHandler() on the typed client (verify the API name for your .NET version).

5 Core focus (exam and interview)
- Most asked: when to use sync vs async, and why call chains hurt availability.
- Know the availability formula: multiply the SLAs of services in series.
- Know that retries need idempotency and a retry limit with backoff and jitter.
- Know the gRPC load balancing problem with long-lived connections.
- Know when the gateway adds value (aggregation, auth, throttling) and when it becomes a bottleneck.
- Defaults to remember: HttpClient default timeout is 100 seconds (verify), which is too long for service calls.

6 Real-world use case
- Situation: an order system in a retail app. Checkout must confirm payment right away, but sending email, updating stock reports and notifying the warehouse can wait.
- Choice made: the Angular app calls a BFF over REST. The BFF calls the Order service and Payment service synchronously over REST with a 3 second timeout. Order then publishes an OrderPlaced event to Service Bus. Email, warehouse and reporting services subscribe to a topic.
- Trade-off: the sync part is limited to two hops, so availability stays high. The cost is eventual consistency, so the warehouse may be a few seconds behind, and you need idempotent consumers.
- When NOT to use it: for a small team with one deployable unit, a modular monolith with in-process calls is simpler and faster. Do not add a broker or gateway before you feel the pain.

7 Interview lens
- Q: Why not make every service call synchronous?
  - A: Availability multiplies down and latency adds up with each hop. One slow service blocks everyone upstream.
- Q: When would you choose gRPC over REST?
  - A: For internal, high-volume, low-latency calls with a strict contract or streaming. Not for public APIs or direct browser use.
- Q: A downstream service is slow. What protects the caller?
  - A: Timeouts, limited retries with backoff and jitter, a circuit breaker, and a fallback or queue. Retry only idempotent calls.
- Q: Orchestration or choreography?
  - A: Orchestration for multi-step flows that need clear visibility and compensation. Choreography for simple fan-out where services stay independent.

8 Common mistakes
- Long synchronous chains. Shows up as slow pages and cascading failure. Fix: cut hops, move non-urgent work to messaging.
- No timeout, or the default 100 seconds. Shows up as thread pool starvation. Fix: set short timeouts per call.
- Retrying everywhere without idempotency. Shows up as duplicate orders or payments. Fix: idempotency keys and retry only safe calls.
- gRPC behind an L4 load balancer. Shows up as one hot pod. Fix: L7 balancing or client-side balancing.
- Breaking changes in place. Shows up as consumers failing after a deploy. Fix: additive changes, versioned endpoints, contract tests.
- Business logic in the gateway. Shows up as a hard-to-change central bottleneck. Fix: keep the gateway to routing, auth and aggregation.

9 Mental model
- Think of phone calls vs letters.
  - Phone call (sync): both must be free at the same moment, and if the line is bad you wait.
  - Letter (async): you drop it in the box and carry on, and the post office holds it.
- Rule of thumb: use a call only when you cannot continue without the answer.

10 Mini lab (30 to 40 minutes, local tools)
- Steps:
  - Create two minimal API projects: Orders (port 5001) and Payments (port 5002) with dotnet new web.
  - In Payments, add an endpoint GET /pay that waits 10 seconds with Task.Delay and then returns 200.
  - In Orders, register a typed HttpClient for Payments with a 2 second Timeout and call /pay from an endpoint.
  - Run both and call Orders. Confirm it fails fast with a timeout instead of hanging.
  - Add AddStandardResilienceHandler() (Microsoft.Extensions.Http.Resilience package) to the client and call again. Watch the logs show retries and the circuit breaker opening after repeated calls.
  - Change /pay in Payments to return 200 after 1 second and confirm Orders succeeds.
- Expected result: the first call fails after about 2 seconds with a timeout. After adding resilience you see retry attempts, then fast failures once the circuit opens.
- Clean-up: stop both apps and delete the two project folders.

11 Revision summary
- Sync chain availability is the product of each service: three at 99.9 percent give about 99.7 percent.
- Use REST for public and browser clients, gRPC for internal and high-volume, messaging for work that can wait.
- Always set a short timeout and retry only idempotent calls with backoff and jitter.
- gRPC needs L7 or client-side load balancing because it reuses one long connection.
- BFF gives each client type its own API shape. Keep business logic out of the gateway.
- Async request-reply: 202 Accepted plus a status URL or correlation ID on a reply queue.
- Orchestration is visible and central. Choreography is loose and harder to trace.
- Make only additive changes so old consumers keep working.
