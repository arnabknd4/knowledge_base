1 What is it
- Anti-patterns are common design mistakes that look reasonable at first and cause pain later. Failure modes are the ways a distributed system breaks when those mistakes meet real load or real faults.
- Distributed monolith: services are deployed separately but cannot change or deploy independently. Nano-services: services so small that the cost of calling them exceeds the value they give. Chatty services: many small calls where one call would do.
- Cascading failure: one slow or failing service drags down its callers, and then their callers. Retry storm: many clients retry at once and overload a service that is already struggling.
- Legacy equivalent: a monolith where one bad stored procedure or one shared DLL could freeze every page, now spread across the network.

2 Why needed
- Most microservice migrations fail from bad boundaries and weak operations, not from bad technology.
- You must spot these patterns early in reviews, because they are far cheaper to fix on a whiteboard than after go-live.
- Without this knowledge you will recommend microservices where a modular monolith would be safer, and you will not explain outages in interviews.
- Architect interviews ask for failure stories and mitigations more than for definitions.

3 Before you read
- Microservices basics: independent deployment, own database per service (see 9.01).
- Synchronous vs asynchronous calls (REST vs messaging).
- Timeouts, retries and circuit breaker as ideas (see 3.04).
- Coupling and cohesion: how much one part depends on another.

4 Features and core concepts
- Distributed monolith: services must be released together or break each other. Why it matters: you pay the cost of distribution and get none of the benefit. Typical causes: shared database, shared libraries, chained synchronous calls.
- Nano-services: one tiny function per service. Why it matters: more deployments, more network hops, more on-call load, no real isolation gain. Fix by merging services around a business capability.
- Chatty services: a client makes many fine-grained calls to build one screen or one result. Why it matters: latency adds up and any single call can fail. Fix with batching, a backend-for-frontend, gateway aggregation or a read model.
- Shared database: several services read and write the same tables. Why it matters: a schema change breaks many teams, and no service truly owns its data. Fix: each service owns its data and exposes an API or events.
- Cascading failures: a slow dependency holds threads and connections in its callers. Why it matters: the whole chain fails, not just one service. Fix with timeouts, circuit breakers, bulkheads and load shedding.
- Retry storms: every layer retries failed calls, so load multiplies (three layers with three retries each can mean 27 calls). Why it matters: retries turn a small slowdown into an outage. Fix: retry at one layer only, use exponential backoff with jitter, add a retry budget, and honor Retry-After.
- Tight coupling through shared libraries: a common NuGet package holds DTOs and business logic used by every service. Why it matters: one change forces all services to upgrade together. Fix: share only stable, tiny contracts or generate clients from OpenAPI; accept some duplication.
- Unclear ownership: nobody owns a service, its alerts or its data. Why it matters: incidents have no owner and the service rots. Fix: one team per service, named on-call, owner field in the service catalog.
- Versioning problems: a breaking API or event change deployed without notice. Why it matters: consumers fail at runtime. Fix: backward-compatible changes, consumer-driven contract tests, a deprecation policy (see 3.07).
- Big-bang rewrite: replace the whole legacy system in one go. Why it matters: long delay, no feedback, and the old system keeps changing. Fix: strangler fig, extract one capability at a time (see 9.03).
- Resume-driven design: choosing Kubernetes, Kafka or microservices because they look good on a CV, not because the problem needs them. Why it matters: team skill and operations cost are ignored. Fix: write an ADR that states the problem, the alternatives and the team capacity.
- Mitigations summary: timeouts everywhere, circuit breaker, bulkhead, backoff with jitter, idempotent consumers, async messaging for non-urgent work, observability, contract tests.
- Setting worth memorizing (Polly resilience via Microsoft.Extensions.Http.Resilience, verify package name):
- builder.Services.AddHttpClient("orders").AddStandardResilienceHandler();
- This adds timeout, retry, circuit breaker and rate limiter with defaults (verify defaults).

5 Core focus (exam and interview)
- Most asked: how do you tell a distributed monolith from real microservices? Test: can one service be deployed and rolled back without touching any other?
- Rule of thumb for call chains: availability multiplies. Five services at 99.9 percent each give about 99.5 percent for the chain.
- Retry rule: retries only for idempotent operations or with idempotency keys.
- Timeout rule: a caller's timeout must be shorter than its own caller's timeout, otherwise work continues after the user gave up.
- Typical exam wording: pick the Azure pattern that stops a failing dependency from exhausting resources (bulkhead or circuit breaker), or that smooths a traffic spike (queue-based load leveling).
- Defaults vary by library, so state the principle and write (verify) for numbers.

6 Real-world use case
- Situation: a Kolkata retail client split a .NET Framework order system into 14 services in six months. Order, Inventory, Pricing and Customer services shared one SQL Server database, and the Order service called the other three synchronously on every request.
- Symptoms: releases needed all four teams together, and a slow Pricing query made checkout time out. Clients retried, load tripled, and the database saturated.
- Choice made: merge Pricing into Inventory (nano-service fix), give each service its own schema with views as a temporary bridge, add timeouts and a circuit breaker on Pricing calls, and publish stock changes through Azure Service Bus so Order uses a local read model.
- Trade-off: more eventual consistency (stock shown can be a few seconds old) and extra work for outbox and idempotent consumers.
- When not to apply this split: a team of fewer than about 10 developers, one release cadence, and no platform or on-call capacity. Use a modular monolith instead.

7 Interview lens
- Q: What is a distributed monolith and how do you detect it?
- A: Separately deployed services that cannot change or release independently. Signs: lockstep releases, shared database, long synchronous chains, shared business libraries.
- Q: How do retry storms start and how do you stop them?
- A: Several layers retry the same failed call and multiply the load. Retry at one layer, use backoff with jitter, add a retry budget and a circuit breaker.
- Q: Is a shared library always bad?
- A: No. Shared infrastructure code such as logging or auth helpers is fine. Shared business logic and shared DTOs couple release cycles, so avoid them.
- Q: Why is a big-bang rewrite risky and what is the alternative?
- A: No value is delivered until the end and requirements keep moving. Use the strangler fig pattern with YARP or API Management to move one capability at a time.

8 Common mistakes
- Splitting by technical layer (UI service, data service) instead of business capability. Shows up as every feature touching every service. Fix with bounded contexts.
- No timeouts on HttpClient calls, so threads wait for the default. Shows up as thread pool starvation during an outage. Fix: explicit timeouts and resilience handlers.
- Retrying non-idempotent calls such as payment. Shows up as double charges. Fix: idempotency keys.
- Treating a message queue as a fix for everything. Shows up as poison messages and hidden backlog. Fix: dead-letter queue, alerts on queue depth, idempotent consumers.
- Ignoring operations cost. Shows up as no tracing, so nobody can find which service is slow. Fix: distributed tracing and correlation IDs before the third service ships.
- Mitigating only in code and not in design. A circuit breaker hides a bad boundary but does not repair it.

9 Mental model
- Think of a row of dominoes tied together with string.
- Dominoes = services. String = hidden coupling (shared database, shared library, sync chain).
- A good design has gaps and cushions between dominoes (timeouts, queues, circuit breakers) so one falling domino does not pull the rest.
- Text sketch: Client -> A -> B -> C -> DB. If C is slow, B waits, A waits, client retries, load grows. Fix: timeout at each hop, breaker on C, queue for non-urgent work.

10 Mini lab
- Time: about 30 minutes. Tools: .NET SDK and two small ASP.NET Core minimal APIs locally, no Azure needed.
- Step 1: create ServiceB with one endpoint /price that sleeps 10 seconds with Task.Delay and returns a number.
- Step 2: create ServiceA with one endpoint /checkout that calls ServiceB using HttpClient with no timeout and no resilience handler.
- Step 3: run both, then send 50 parallel requests to /checkout (for example with a simple loop of curl in the background). Observe that requests hang and ServiceA slows down.
- Step 4: add AddStandardResilienceHandler() to the ServiceA HttpClient registration (verify package Microsoft.Extensions.Http.Resilience) and set a 2 second attempt timeout.
- Step 5: repeat the load. Expected result: ServiceA fails fast, the circuit opens after repeated failures, and ServiceA stays responsive.
- Step 6: change ServiceA to return a fallback price when the call fails, and confirm /checkout still answers.
- Clean-up: stop both apps, delete the two project folders.

11 Revision summary
- Distributed monolith test: can one service deploy alone without breaking others?
- Shared database and shared business libraries are the two main hidden couplings.
- Chained sync calls multiply failure: availability is the product of each service's availability.
- Retries multiply load: retry at one layer, with backoff, jitter and a budget.
- Cascading failure is stopped by timeouts, circuit breaker, bulkhead and load shedding.
- Big-bang rewrite is replaced by the strangler fig approach.
- Every service needs one owning team, one on-call and its own data.
- Do not split into microservices without team size, independent release need and operations capacity.
