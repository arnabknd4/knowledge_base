9.02 Decomposition and strategic DDD [P2 R L]

1 What is it
- Strategic DDD is a way to split a large system into parts by business meaning, not by technical layers. The main unit is the bounded context: a boundary inside which every word has one exact meaning.
- Ubiquitous language: the shared vocabulary that business people and developers both use, in meetings and in code, inside one bounded context.
- Bounded context: a boundary (team, codebase, model) where one model and one language apply. "Customer" in Sales is not the same thing as "Customer" in Billing.
- Context map: a diagram of all bounded contexts and the relationship type between each pair.
- Subdomain: a part of the business problem. Core subdomain gives competitive advantage. Supporting subdomain is needed but not special. Generic subdomain is solved the same way by everyone (identity, email, payments).
- Aggregate: a cluster of objects changed together as one unit, with one root. Its boundary is the transaction boundary.
- Legacy equivalent: the one big shared EDMX or DbContext with a single Customer class used by every module of the MVC monolith.

2 Why needed
- Splitting by tables, layers or guesswork gives services that call each other constantly (distributed monolith).
- Without clear boundaries, one change to "Order" forces changes in five modules and five teams.
- Without a shared language, developers build what they understood, not what the business meant.
- It tells you where to spend design effort (core) and where to buy or keep it simple (generic).

3 Before you read
- Monolith vs microservices (9.01): why splitting has a cost.
- Coupling and cohesion: parts that change together should live together.
- Basic OOP: entity, class, object.
- Database per service idea: each service owns its data.

4 Features and core concepts
- Bounded context: one model, one language, one owner. Why it matters: it is the natural candidate for a service or a module in a modular monolith.
- Ubiquitous language: words used the same in talk, docs and class names. Why it matters: when the code says "Shipment" but the business says "Dispatch", bugs hide in the gap.
- Context map: drawing of contexts and their relationships. Why it matters: it shows who depends on whom, and where integration pain will be.
- Shared kernel: two contexts share a small common model and code. Why it matters: fast, but changes need both teams to agree. Use sparingly.
- Customer-supplier: upstream context supplies, downstream consumes, and downstream needs are planned by upstream. Why it matters: gives the downstream team a voice.
- Conformist: downstream accepts the upstream model as is, with no influence. Why it matters: cheap, but you inherit their model and their changes.
- Anti-corruption layer (ACL): a translation layer that protects your model from a foreign or legacy one. Why it matters: stops legacy concepts leaking into new code. Core tool in strangler migrations (9.03).
- Core, supporting, generic subdomains: classification by business value. Why it matters: build core in-house with best people, keep supporting simple, buy or use SaaS for generic.
- Event storming: a workshop where experts and developers stick orange notes for domain events on a wall and arrange them in time order. Why it matters: finds boundaries, hot spots and language conflicts fast.
- Aggregate boundaries: what must be consistent inside one transaction. Why it matters: one transaction per aggregate; talk to other aggregates by id and events.
- Service granularity: how big one service is. Why it matters: too small gives chatty nano-services, too big gives a mini-monolith. Start from a bounded context, not a noun.
- Data ownership: each context owns its data and exposes it only by API or events. Why it matters: a shared database breaks every boundary you drew.
- Team alignment: one team owns one context end to end (Conway's law, used on purpose). Why it matters: boundaries that cut across teams do not survive.
- Worth memorizing: Context map notation: Upstream (U) -> Downstream (D); label the arrow: ACL, CF (conformist), CS (customer-supplier), SK (shared kernel).

5 Core focus (exam and interview)
- Most asked: what is a bounded context, how do you find one, and is it the same as a microservice (no: it is a logical boundary; a service is one way to deploy it).
- Know the context map patterns by name and when to use each.
- Know the three subdomain types and the build vs buy rule for each.
- Typical rule of thumb: one aggregate per transaction; references between aggregates by id.
- A bounded context can be a module in a modular monolith first. Splitting to a service is a later, separate decision.
- Subdomains are in the problem space (business). Bounded contexts are in the solution space (software). They often align, but not always.

6 Real-world use case
- Situation: a legacy .NET Framework MVC insurance app, one Customer table used by Sales, Claims, Billing and Support. Every release breaks something else.
- Choice: run an event storming session with business users. Find three contexts: Policy Sales (core), Claims (core), Billing (supporting). Identity and notification are generic, so use Entra External ID and Azure Communication Services (verify). Billing stays a module for now. Claims is extracted first behind YARP, with an ACL translating the old Customer record into a Claims Claimant model. Each context gets its own database.
- Trade-off: a lot of upfront workshop time and duplicated data (a customer name exists in three places, synced by events). You trade a single easy query for independent releases.
- When NOT to use: a small CRUD app, a team under about 8 to 10 people, or a domain nobody understands yet. Full strategic DDD there is overhead. Use a well-structured modular monolith.

7 Interview lens
- Q: What is a bounded context?
  A: A boundary where one domain model and one language are valid. The same word can mean different things in different contexts.
- Q: Bounded context vs microservice?
  A: A context is a logical and ownership boundary. A service is a deployment choice. One context can be one service, or a module in a monolith.
- Q: When do you use an anti-corruption layer?
  A: When integrating with a legacy or external model you do not want to adopt. The ACL translates both ways so your model stays clean.
- Q: How do you decide service size?
  A: Start from a bounded context and its team. Check for chatty calls and cross-service transactions. If they appear, the boundary is wrong.

8 Common mistakes
- Splitting by entity (CustomerService, OrderService, ProductService). Shows up as every use case needing five calls. Fix: split by business capability and language.
- Skipping the business people. Shows up as models that match the database, not the business. Fix: run event storming with domain experts.
- Sharing one database "for now". Shows up as hidden coupling and blocked schema changes. Fix: data ownership per context, access through API or events.
- Shared kernel everywhere. Shows up as lockstep releases. Fix: prefer separate models with an ACL or published language.
- Over-engineering generic subdomains. Shows up as months spent on a homemade login. Fix: buy or use a platform service.
- Aggregates too big. Shows up as lock contention and slow saves. Fix: smaller aggregates, eventual consistency between them.

9 Mental model
- A city map. Each bounded context is a neighbourhood with its own local dialect. The context map is the road and bridge plan. The ACL is a border translator. Aggregates are single houses: you lock one door at a time.

   [Sales] --CS--> [Billing] --ACL--> [Legacy ERP]
      |
      +--events--> [Claims]

10 Mini lab (30 to 40 minutes, paper plus free tools)
- Pick the legacy app you know best.
- Step 1: on paper, write 15 to 20 domain events in past tense (PolicyIssued, ClaimFiled, PaymentReceived) on sticky notes or lines.
- Step 2: order them left to right in time. Mark where the same word means different things (for example "Customer").
- Step 3: draw boundaries around groups of events that use one language. Name each context.
- Step 4: classify each as core, supporting or generic.
- Step 5: draw a context map with arrows (upstream to downstream) and label each with ACL, CS, CF or SK.
- Step 6: for the first context to extract, list its aggregate and what data it owns.
- Expected result: one page with 3 to 5 contexts, labelled relationships, and one chosen first extraction.
- Clean-up: photograph the page, store it in your capstone repo under docs, and discard the sticky notes.

11 Revision summary
- Bounded context = one model, one language, one owner.
- Ubiquitous language lives in talk and in code.
- Context map patterns: shared kernel, customer-supplier, conformist, anti-corruption layer.
- Subdomains: core (build best), supporting (keep simple), generic (buy).
- Event storming finds boundaries and language conflicts.
- Aggregate = transaction boundary; reference other aggregates by id.
- A context is not automatically a microservice.
- Each context owns its data; never share a database.
