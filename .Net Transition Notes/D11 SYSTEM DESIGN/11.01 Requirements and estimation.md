1 What is it
- Requirements are what the system must do (functional) and how well it must do it (non-functional). Estimation is rough arithmetic to size traffic, storage and bandwidth before you pick a design.
- A quality attribute is a measurable property such as availability or latency. An SLI (service level indicator) is the measurement. An SLO (service level objective) is the target for it.
- Back-of-envelope math means quick, approximate numbers (QPS = queries per second) that tell you whether one server or one hundred are needed.
- Legacy equivalent: the sizing and "expected load" section of a project proposal or capacity sheet, and the NFR lines you wrote for IIS plus SQL Server deployments.

2 Why needed
- Designs fail on unstated requirements, not on missing features. "Fast" and "highly available" mean nothing until they have numbers.
- Without estimation you either over-build (cost) or under-build (outage on launch day).
- Interviews test this first. A design that starts with numbers shows architect-level thinking.
- It gives you a basis to justify every later choice: cache, queue, shard, replica.

3 Before you read
- Basic units: KB, MB, GB, TB, and powers of 10 versus 2.
- What a request, a database row and a payload are.
- The difference between a user, a session and a request.
- Percentages and averages (percentiles are explained in section 4).

4 Features and core concepts
- Functional vs non-functional: functional is what the system does (place an order). Non-functional is how well (order placed in under 300 ms). Why it matters: architecture is driven mostly by non-functional needs.
- Availability: the share of time the system works. Why it matters: 99.9 percent allows about 8.8 hours of downtime a year, 99.99 percent about 53 minutes.
- Latency: time for one request, measured as percentiles (p95 means 95 percent of requests are faster). Why it matters: averages hide the slow tail users feel.
- Throughput: work done per unit time, such as requests per second. Why it matters: it sets how many instances you need.
- Consistency: whether all readers see the latest write. Why it matters: strong consistency costs latency and availability.
- Durability: committed data is not lost. Why it matters: it decides replication and backup design.
- Scalability: ability to handle growth by adding resources. Why it matters: you must say what grows (users, data, traffic).
- Security: confidentiality, integrity and access control needs. Why it matters: it changes the design early (private endpoints, encryption), not at the end.
- Cost: the budget ceiling per month or per user. Why it matters: every quality attribute above can be bought with money, so cost is the trade-off partner.
- Operability: how easy it is to deploy, monitor and fix. Why it matters: a design nobody can run at 3 AM is a bad design.
- SLI and SLO: SLI example is the share of requests under 300 ms. SLO example is 99 percent of them over 30 days. Why it matters: it turns vague goals into testable ones. (An SLA is the contract with penalties; an SLO is the internal target and should be stricter.)
- Back-of-envelope math: convert users into QPS, storage and bandwidth. Why it matters: it picks the architecture size.
- Read-write ratio: reads divided by writes. Why it matters: 100:1 read-heavy points to caching and replicas; write-heavy points to partitioning and queues.
- Peak vs average: peak traffic is often 2 to 10 times the average. Why it matters: you size for peak, not average.
- Numbers to memorize:
  - 1 day = 86,400 seconds, call it 100,000 for quick math
  - 1 million requests per day is about 12 per second
  - QPS = daily requests / 86,400
  - storage = items per day x size x retention days
  - bandwidth = QPS x payload size
  - memory read about 100 ns, SSD read about 100 microseconds, same-region network round trip about 0.5 ms, cross-continent round trip about 150 ms (all approximate, verify)

5 Core focus (exam and interview)
- Interviewers expect you to ask clarifying questions first, then state numbers, then design.
- Most asked: derive QPS and storage from user counts, and say whether the system is read-heavy or write-heavy.
- Expect to translate "99.9 percent" into minutes of downtime.
- Composite SLA: two dependencies in series at 99.9 percent each give about 99.8 percent (multiply them). Parallel redundancy raises it.
- Defaults to quote: peak is 2x to 5x average if not told otherwise (state it as an assumption). Round numbers aggressively; accuracy within a factor of 2 is fine.
- Always state assumptions aloud and write them down.

6 Real-world use case
- Situation: you are designing an order service for an e-commerce client moving off an IIS monolith. Business says 2 million orders per day on sale days and "must never go down".
- Estimation: 2 million / 86,400 is about 23 orders per second average. Peak at 5x is about 115 per second. Each order is about 5 KB, so 10 GB per day, about 3.6 TB per year.
- Requirements pinned: availability 99.95 percent, order placement p95 under 500 ms, no lost orders (durability), reads (order status) about 10x writes.
- Choice made: Azure SQL with zone redundancy for orders, Redis cache for status reads, Service Bus to decouple payment and email steps.
- Trade-off: zone redundancy and Service Bus add cost and operational parts, in exchange for meeting the 99.95 target.
- When NOT to over-engineer: at 23 per second, you do not need sharding or Kafka. One well-indexed Azure SQL database handles it.

7 Interview lens
- Q: What do you ask before designing?
  A: Users, traffic and growth, read-write ratio, latency and availability targets, data size and retention, consistency needs. Then I state assumptions and confirm them.
- Q: How do you turn 10 million daily users into QPS?
  A: Estimate requests per user per day, multiply, divide by 86,400, then apply a peak factor. Example: 10 requests each gives 100 million per day, about 1,150 QPS average, about 5,000 at peak.
- Q: Why percentiles and not averages?
  A: Averages hide the slow tail. One user in twenty seeing 3 seconds is a real problem that p95 and p99 expose.
- Q: Difference between SLI, SLO and SLA?
  A: SLI is the measurement, SLO is the internal target on it, SLA is the external contract with consequences. Keep the SLO stricter than the SLA.

8 Common mistakes
- Designing before asking questions: you solve the wrong problem. Fix: spend the first five minutes on requirements.
- Using average load for sizing: the system falls over at peak. Fix: apply a peak factor and state it.
- Vague goals like "fast" or "highly available": nobody can test them. Fix: attach a number and a window (p95 under 300 ms over 30 days).
- False precision: spending ten minutes on exact math. Fix: round and aim for the right order of magnitude.
- Ignoring cost and operability: the design works but is unaffordable or unrunnable. Fix: list them as attributes and trade them explicitly.
- Setting an SLO equal to the SLA: no safety margin. Fix: set the internal target stricter.

9 Mental model
- Requirements are the question, estimation is the ruler, architecture is the answer.
- Users -> requests per day -> QPS (average, then peak) -> data per day -> storage and bandwidth -> instance count and design choices.
- Every quality attribute pulls against another: more availability costs money, more consistency costs latency.

10 Mini lab (about 30 minutes, paper plus a spreadsheet, no cloud cost)
- Step 1: pick a service you know, such as a leave management or order system. Write 5 clarifying questions and invent answers (users, requests per user, payload size, retention, availability target).
- Step 2: in Excel or Google Sheets, make columns: users, requests per user per day, daily requests, average QPS (/86400), peak QPS (x5), payload KB, daily GB, yearly TB, outbound bandwidth in MB per second at peak.
- Step 3: write three SLIs and SLOs, for example "p95 latency under 300 ms", "99.9 percent success over 30 days", "no committed order lost".
- Step 4: convert your availability target to downtime minutes per month (99.9 percent is about 43 minutes).
- Step 5: write one sentence on whether the system is read-heavy or write-heavy and what that suggests (cache, replica, queue).
- Expected result: one sheet with numbers you can explain in two minutes, and a clear "one database is enough" or "we need scaling" conclusion.
- Clean-up: none needed. Save the sheet; reuse it for 11.05 and 11.06.

11 Revision summary
- Functional is what, non-functional is how well; architecture follows the second.
- Nine attributes: availability, latency, throughput, consistency, durability, scalability, security, cost, operability.
- SLI measures, SLO targets, SLA is the contract; keep SLO stricter than SLA.
- QPS = daily requests / 86,400; 1 million per day is about 12 per second.
- Size for peak (2x to 10x average), not average.
- Read-write ratio decides cache, replica or partition choices.
- 99.9 percent is about 8.8 hours per year; 99.99 percent is about 53 minutes.
- Serial dependencies multiply availability; always say your assumptions aloud.
