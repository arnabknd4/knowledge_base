Topic 11.07 (Design communication) is tagged P3 R L with no K, so this is a FULL note with a mini lab.

1 What is it
- Design communication is the skill of explaining a system design clearly to other people, in an interview or in a real design review.
- It covers how you structure the whiteboard, which questions you ask first, how you state trade-offs, and how you manage the 45 minutes.
- The design itself can be good, but if nobody can follow it, it does not count.
- Legacy equivalent: the architecture walkthrough you gave a client or the offshore team over a Visio diagram and a Word design document.

2 Why needed
- Interviewers judge your thinking process, not only the final diagram.
- Without structure, candidates jump into boxes and arrows, miss requirements, and run out of time before the deep dive.
- In real work, an unclear design review leads to rework, wrong estimates and decisions nobody remembers.
- A lead can code the solution. An architect must also persuade others that it is the right solution.

3 Before you read
- Know the building blocks from 11.01 and 11.02 (requirements, estimation, load balancing, caching, queues).
- Know the quality attributes: availability, latency, consistency, cost.
- Know what an ADR is (15.01), because decision recording links to this topic.
- Know basic box-and-arrow diagramming: client, service, database, queue.

4 Features and core concepts
- Whiteboard structure: a fixed order you follow every time. Requirements, API, data model, high-level design, deep dive, bottlenecks, trade-offs. It matters because the interviewer can always see where you are.
- Clarifying questions: questions you ask before designing. Who are the users, how many, read-heavy or write-heavy, what must never fail. They matter because the prompt is deliberately vague, and wrong assumptions ruin the design.
- Articulating trade-offs: saying what you chose, what you gave up and why. Example: "I choose eventual consistency here, so the feed may be a few seconds stale, but writes stay fast." It matters because every choice has a cost, and naming it shows seniority.
- Diagram conventions: one shape per kind of thing, labelled arrows, a legend if needed. Left to right for the request flow. Number the steps. It matters because a messy diagram hides good thinking.
- Estimation narrative: say your numbers aloud while calculating. State assumptions, round aggressively, and give the result as QPS, storage and bandwidth. It matters because the interviewer checks reasoning, not exact arithmetic.
- 45-minute time boxing: a split such as 5 minutes requirements, 5 estimation and API, 10 high-level design, 15 deep dive, 5 bottlenecks and trade-offs, 5 questions. It matters because most candidates spend too long on the first diagram.
- Common mistakes: starting without questions, designing for Google scale with no reason, silence while thinking, ignoring the interviewer's hints, never mentioning failure. It matters because these cost more than a wrong technology choice.
- Justifying decisions: tie each choice to a stated requirement. Say "because the read-write ratio is 100 to 1, I add a cache". It matters because a choice with no reason looks like memorized buzzwords.
- Running a design review: for real work, share the document beforehand, state the goal and the decision needed, collect objections, record the outcome in an ADR. It matters because a review without a recorded decision is repeated in the next meeting.
- Worth memorizing, the fixed order: requirements, API, data model, high-level design, deep dive, bottlenecks, trade-offs.

5 Core focus (exam and interview)
- Most asked: "Walk me through how you would approach this design." Answer with the fixed order, not with a technology.
- Interviewers watch for: clarifying questions, numbers backed by assumptions, explicit trade-offs, and handling of failure.
- Limit to remember: 45 minutes. Aim to start the deep dive by minute 20 to 25.
- Say what you will not cover, so the interviewer can redirect you.
- Pick the deep dive where the real difficulty is (hot partition, double booking, idempotency), not the easiest part.

6 Real-world use case
- Situation: your team wants to replace the IIS-hosted order module with a service on Azure Container Apps using Service Bus. The client architect and the offshore team lead have different views.
- Choice made: you send a two-page RFC a day early with goals, options, a comparison on cost, risk and team skill, and your recommendation. In the review you state the decision needed, take objections, and write an ADR with status and consequences.
- Trade-off: the RFC and review cost about a day of effort, but they prevent weeks of rework and a re-opened debate.
- When NOT to use it: for small, reversible decisions (a library choice inside one service), a full review is overhead. A short message in the pull request is enough.

7 Interview lens
- Q: How do you start a system design question?
  A: I ask clarifying questions on users, scale, read-write ratio and must-have features. Then I write down functional and non-functional requirements and confirm them.
- Q: How do you handle a trade-off in the design?
  A: I name the options, state what each costs, and choose one by linking it to a stated requirement. I also say when I would change the choice.
- Q: You are stuck mid-design. What do you do?
  A: I say what I am stuck on, state my assumption, and move forward. I invite the interviewer's hint instead of going silent.
- Q: How do you run a design review with a disagreeing team?
  A: I share the document early and frame the decision needed. I compare options on agreed criteria and record the outcome in an ADR.

8 Common mistakes
- Jumping to boxes in the first two minutes. It shows as a design that misses a requirement found later. Fix: spend the first five minutes on questions.
- Over-engineering with Kafka, sharding and multi-region for 100 requests per second. It shows as a justification you cannot defend. Fix: size first, then add components only when the numbers demand them.
- Thinking silently. The interviewer cannot score what they cannot hear. Fix: narrate short thoughts.
- Never saying the downside of a choice. It reads as junior. Fix: attach one cost to every decision.
- Running out of time before the deep dive. Fix: keep a visible time split and check the clock at each phase.
- Design review with no recorded decision. It shows as the same debate again next month. Fix: write an ADR before the meeting ends.

9 Mental model
- A guided tour, not a lecture. You are the tour guide and the interviewer is the visitor.

  Questions -> Numbers -> API -> Data -> Boxes -> Hard part -> Failures -> Trade-offs
  (agree on the route)  (walk it in order)   (say where it can break and what it costs)

10 Mini lab (30 minutes)
- Tools: a timer, a sheet of paper or a free whiteboard tool, no Azure needed.
- Steps:
  1. Pick one prompt from 11.05, for example the URL shortener.
  2. Set a timer for 45 minutes. Say everything aloud, or record your voice on your phone.
  3. Minutes 0 to 5: write five clarifying questions and your assumed answers.
  4. Minutes 5 to 10: estimate QPS and storage aloud, showing assumptions.
  5. Minutes 10 to 20: draw the high-level diagram with numbered request steps.
  6. Minutes 20 to 35: deep dive on one hard part, for example unique ID generation.
  7. Minutes 35 to 45: list bottlenecks and three trade-offs, each with its cost.
- Expected result: one page with the fixed order visible, a numbers section, a labelled diagram, and at least three trade-offs with costs. In the recording, you should hear no silence longer than about 10 seconds.
- Clean-up: delete the recording, keep the page in your binder, and note the phase where you ran over time.

11 Revision summary
- Fixed order: requirements, API, data model, high-level design, deep dive, bottlenecks, trade-offs.
- Always ask clarifying questions first, about five minutes.
- Every decision gets a reason tied to a requirement and a stated cost.
- Say estimation assumptions aloud and round the numbers.
- Time box for 45 minutes: start the deep dive by minute 20 to 25.
- Most common mistakes: no questions, over-engineering, silence, no failure discussion.
- A design review ends with a recorded decision in an ADR.
