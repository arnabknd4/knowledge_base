1 What is it
- An interview preparation map is a plan of which interview rounds you will face and what each round tests. It also holds a mock interview schedule and a question bank for each domain.
- Rounds for a lead moving to architect roles: .NET deep dive, SQL, system design, cloud scenario, pipeline design, behavioral, managerial (team, estimation, conflict), architect (trade-offs), client-facing.
- Think of it as the exam timetable for a job search: it tells you what to revise before each paper.
- Legacy equivalent: the informal habit of "revise everything before the interview", replaced by a round-by-round plan.

2 Why needed
- Different companies test different rounds. Without a map you revise everything evenly and miss the rounds that decide the offer.
- Lead and architect interviews test judgment, not only facts. A map reminds you to prepare trade-off stories and design talk, not just .NET trivia.
- Without a mock schedule, the first real interview becomes your practice run.
- Without a question bank, weak spots stay hidden until you are asked in front of a panel.

3 Before you read
- Your story bank (17.01) has eight to ten STAR stories. STAR means Situation, Task, Action, Result.
- Your resume (17.03) is ready, because interviewers ask from it.
- You know the domain topics in Part 4 at least once. The map uses them, it does not teach them.
- The capstone project (17.02) exists, because several rounds will ask about it.

4 Features and core concepts
- Round: .NET deep dive. Plain meaning: questions on DI, async, EF Core, middleware, memory, configuration. Why it matters: it is the first filter, and weak basics end the interview early.
- Round: SQL. Plain meaning: indexing, query plans, transactions, isolation, locking. Why it matters: lead roles own database performance, and a slow query is the usual production fire.
- Round: system design. Plain meaning: a 45-minute open problem such as a URL shortener or an order system. Why it matters: it is the main architect filter. They watch how you ask questions and justify choices.
- Round: cloud scenario. Plain meaning: "Move this IIS app to Azure" or "Make this highly available". Why it matters: it checks that you can pick the right Azure service and explain cost and risk.
- Round: pipeline design. Plain meaning: design CI/CD for a service, with tests, scans, approvals, rollback. Why it matters: your GitLab background helps, but they expect Azure DevOps or GitHub Actions words.
- Round: behavioral. Plain meaning: past situations answered with STAR stories. Why it matters: it checks maturity, ownership and how you handle failure.
- Round: managerial (team, estimation, conflict). Plain meaning: how you plan, estimate, delegate and resolve disagreement. Why it matters: a lead is hired for people and delivery as much as code.
- Round: architect (trade-offs). Plain meaning: "Why this and not that?" on microservices vs monolith, SQL vs NoSQL, sync vs async. Why it matters: the answer matters less than the reasoning, risk and cost you show.
- Round: client-facing. Plain meaning: explaining a design or a delay to a non-technical person. Why it matters: service firms check whether you can face a client without a manager.
- Mock interview schedule. Plain meaning: fixed dates for practice interviews with a friend, a colleague or an AI. Why it matters: speaking aloud is a different skill from reading.
- Question bank per domain. Plain meaning: one list of questions for each of D01 to D17, built from the interview lens section of each note. Why it matters: it is your single revision list and it grows with every note.
- Worth memorizing: a round tracker line, "Round | Topics to revise | Story to use | Last mock date".

5 Core focus (exam and interview)
- Most asked for architect roles: system design, trade-off reasoning, cloud scenarios, then .NET and SQL depth.
- Panels often combine rounds. A technical round may include SQL, design and a behavioral question in the same hour.
- Time box for a design round is about 45 minutes. Spend about 5 on requirements, 10 on high-level design, 20 on deep dive, 10 on trade-offs.
- Expect to be asked about your own project in every round. Know its numbers, decisions and failures.
- Prepare per company type: product firms lean on design and depth, service firms lean on delivery, client handling and estimation (verify against each job posting).

6 Real-world use case
- Situation: you have two interviews next month, one at a Kolkata service firm and one at a remote product company.
- Choice: write the round list for each from the job posting and recruiter call, then map each round to topic IDs from Part 4 and a story from your bank. Schedule one mock per round in the last two weeks.
- Trade-off: this costs a weekend to set up and takes time from new notes. The gain is that revision is targeted.
- When NOT to use it: do not build a map for every posting. If a round list is unknown, ask the recruiter first. Do not let the map replace actual study of the domains.

7 Interview lens
- Q: How do you prepare for an architect interview? A: I map the rounds, then link each round to my notes and a project story. I run timed mocks for system design.
- Q: How do you prepare for a round you have not faced before? A: I ask the recruiter what the round covers, then write ten likely questions and answer them aloud. I get feedback from a mock.
- Q: How do you handle a question you do not know? A: I state what I know, name my assumption, and reason step by step. I do not bluff, and I say how I would verify.
- Q: What do you do after a failed interview? A: I write the questions I missed the same day and add them to the question bank. I schedule a mock on that weak area.

8 Common mistakes
- Revising only .NET trivia. Shows up as strong code answers and weak design answers. Fix: give design and trade-off rounds equal weekly time.
- No spoken practice. Shows up as knowing the answer but rambling. Fix: answer aloud, with a timer, at least once per topic.
- One generic story for every behavioral question. Shows up as repeated answers. Fix: match different stories from the bank to different questions.
- Ignoring client-facing and managerial rounds. Shows up as rejection after strong technical rounds. Fix: rehearse estimation, conflict and delay-explanation answers.
- A question bank that is never updated. Shows up as the same gaps across interviews. Fix: add every missed question within a day.

9 Mental model
- Think of it as a tracker with three columns per round.

Round -> what they test -> my proof (note, story or project)

- If the proof column is empty for a round, that round is your next study target.

10 Mini lab
- Time: 30 minutes. Tools: a plain text file or paper.
- Step 1: take one real job posting you like. Copy its required skills.
- Step 2: list the likely rounds from the nine rounds in section 4. Cross out those the posting does not suggest.
- Step 3: for each remaining round, write the topic IDs from Part 4 that feed it.
- Step 4: next to each round, write one story from your story bank or one capstone fact you would use.
- Step 5: fill the proof column. Mark every round with no proof in red.
- Step 6: pick three dates in the next two weeks for mocks on your three weakest rounds.
- Expected result: one page showing rounds, linked topics, proof and mock dates, with your weakest rounds visible.
- Clean-up: file the page at the front of the D17 section of your binder.

11 Revision summary
- There are nine rounds: .NET, SQL, system design, cloud scenario, pipeline design, behavioral, managerial, architect, client-facing.
- Each round needs topic IDs, a story and a mock date.
- A system design round runs about 45 minutes and rewards reasoning over the final diagram.
- Ask the recruiter for the round list before preparing.
- Speak answers aloud. Reading is not practice.
- The question bank is one list per domain, built from interview lens sections.
- Add every missed question to the bank within a day.
- An empty proof column marks your next study target.
