1 What is it
- Agile and delivery management is how a team plans, runs and tracks work so software ships predictably.
- Scrum works in fixed sprints (usually 2 weeks) with set roles and meetings. Kanban works as a continuous flow with limits on work in progress.
- Delivery management covers the commercial side: client SLAs, change requests and pricing models.
- Backlog: the ordered list of work waiting to be done.
- Sprint: a fixed time box in which the team commits to a set of backlog items.
- Cycle time: elapsed time from starting an item to finishing it.
- Velocity: story points the team completes per sprint, used for planning only.
- SLA: a written promise to the client about response or resolution time.
- Legacy equivalent: waterfall plans with a fixed scope document and a Gantt chart, plus the change control board in older client projects.

2 Why needed
- Requirements change while work is in progress. Fixed up-front plans break when they do.
- Without a shared definition of ready and done, work reaches testing half-finished and rework piles up.
- Without release planning and risk tracking, the client learns about delays only on the due date.
- Without change request discipline, scope grows silently and the margin disappears on a fixed-price contract.
- A lead or architect is judged on predictable delivery, not only on good code.

3 Before you read

- Your own experience of sprints, stand-ups and sprint reviews in Infosys-style delivery.
- What a client contract is: scope, price, duration.

4 Features and core concepts
- Scrum: a framework with three roles (product owner, scrum master, developers), fixed sprints and review meetings. It gives a steady rhythm and a regular chance to change direction.
- Kanban: a board with columns and work-in-progress limits. It suits support and maintenance work where requests arrive randomly, because nothing waits for a sprint boundary.
- Backlog refinement: the team reviews upcoming items, splits large ones, clarifies questions and estimates them. It keeps sprint planning short and stops surprises mid-sprint.
- Sprint planning: the team picks items it can finish in the sprint and sets a sprint goal. It makes the commitment realistic and visible.
- Definition of ready: the checklist an item must meet before the team accepts it (clear acceptance criteria, dependencies known, sized). It stops vague items from entering a sprint.
- Definition of done: the checklist an item must meet to count as finished (code reviewed, tests passing, deployed to test, documented). It stops "90% done" items.
- Cycle time and velocity: cycle time shows how fast single items flow, velocity shows capacity per sprint. Use both for forecasting. Never use velocity to compare teams or rate people.
- Release planning: map sprints to releases and milestones, with scope buffers and dependencies. It tells the client what to expect and when.
- SAFe awareness: a framework for scaling agile across many teams, with program increments (about 8 to 12 weeks) and release trains. Know the vocabulary because large Indian service clients use it. (verify current SAFe version)
- Risk and issue tracking: a register with owner, probability, impact and mitigation for risks, and owner and due date for issues. A risk is something that may happen. An issue has already happened.
- Client SLAs: agreed response and resolution times by severity, with penalties or credits. They set what the support team must meet and how it is measured.
- Change requests: a formal record that scope, time or cost is changing, with impact analysis and client approval. It protects the team from unpaid scope growth.
- Time-and-material vs fixed price: T&M bills for effort used, so the client carries scope risk. Fixed price bills a set amount for set scope, so the vendor carries estimate risk.
- Worth memorizing: Kanban rule is "stop starting, start finishing", and forecast with Little's Law: average cycle time = work in progress / throughput.

5 Core focus (exam and interview)
- Most asked: difference between Scrum and Kanban, and when you would choose each.
- Most asked: what goes into definition of ready versus definition of done.
- Most asked: how you handle scope change on a fixed-price project.
- Most asked: how you use velocity, and why it must not be a performance target.
- Defaults to remember: sprint is 1 to 4 weeks (commonly 2). Sprint goal is set at planning. Retrospective happens after every sprint.
- Scrum events: planning, daily scrum (15 minutes), review, retrospective. (verify against current Scrum Guide)
- Kanban has no fixed iterations and no mandatory roles. WIP limits are its core control.
- Fixed price needs a strict change request process. T&M needs good time reporting and regular budget visibility.

6 Real-world use case
- Situation: a client in a Kolkata-based delivery centre runs a fixed-price modernization of a .NET Framework app. Midway, the client asks for extra reports and a new login method.
- Choice made: log both as change requests, estimate impact in days and cost, and let the client decide. Keep the sprint goal protected. Add approved work to the backlog with a clear priority.
- Trade-off: formal change control slows the client's requests and can strain the relationship. It also protects margin and keeps the schedule honest.
- Run support work (production bugs) on a Kanban board with WIP limits and SLA clocks, separate from the Scrum feature team.
- When NOT to use it: do not run heavy Scrum ceremonies for a two-person team doing a one-week fix. Do not use fixed price when requirements are unclear. Choose T&M or a time-boxed discovery phase first.

7 Interview lens
- Q: When would you pick Kanban over Scrum?
- A: Pick Kanban for support and maintenance work with unpredictable arrivals. Pick Scrum when the team builds planned features and benefits from fixed cadence.
- Q: A client adds scope mid-sprint on a fixed-price project. What do you do?
- A: Do not change the sprint silently. Raise a change request with impact on time and cost, get approval, then schedule it by priority.
- Q: How do you use velocity?
- A: Use the average of recent sprints to forecast release dates. Never compare teams or set it as a target, because teams will inflate estimates.
- Q: How do you keep a client informed on risk?
- A: Keep a risk register with owners and mitigation. Review it weekly and report top risks in the status update with decisions needed.

8 Common mistakes
- Treating velocity as a target. Teams inflate points and real output does not rise. Fix: use it only for forecasting.
- Weak definition of done. Items are "done" but untested, and bugs surface in the release week. Fix: write it down, include testing and deployment, and enforce it in review.
- Taking vague items into a sprint. The sprint misses its goal. Fix: apply definition of ready in refinement.
- Skipping change requests to keep the client happy. Margin shrinks and the team burns out. Fix: log every change, even small ones.
- Running Kanban without WIP limits. Everything is in progress and nothing finishes. Fix: set limits and enforce them.
- Mixing support and feature work in one sprint. Urgent bugs wreck the sprint plan. Fix: separate the flows or reserve fixed capacity.

9 Mental model
- Think of delivery as a pipe with a tap (backlog) and a gate (done):

  Backlog -> Ready -> In progress (WIP limit) -> Done -> Release
  Scope change enters only through a change request.
  Risks and SLAs sit beside the pipe as alarms.

10 Mini lab
- Time: 30 minutes. Tools: a free Azure DevOps organization (Boards) or paper and sticky notes.
- Step 1: create a project and add 8 work items for a small .NET API (for example, login, list orders, export report).
- Step 2: write one definition of ready and one definition of done as 4 bullets each in the project wiki.
- Step 3: set up a Kanban board with columns New, Ready, Doing, Done. Set a WIP limit of 2 on Doing.
- Step 4: move 3 items to Doing and observe the limit warning. Finish one item before starting the next.
- Step 5: add one change request as a new work item with estimate and impact notes.
- Expected result: board shows the WIP warning, the cumulative flow chart starts to build, and the change request sits in the backlog unestimated into the sprint.
- Clean-up: delete the test project in Azure DevOps project settings.

11 Revision summary
- Scrum is fixed sprints with roles and events. Kanban is continuous flow with WIP limits.
- Definition of ready gates entry. Definition of done gates exit.
- Velocity is for forecasting only, never for comparing people or teams.
- Cycle time is start to finish of one item. Little's Law links WIP, throughput and cycle time.
- Risk is a possible event. Issue is an event that has happened.
- Every scope change on a fixed-price contract needs a change request with impact and approval.
- Fixed price puts estimate risk on the vendor. T&M puts scope risk on the client.
- SAFe scales agile across teams using program increments. Know the vocabulary only.
