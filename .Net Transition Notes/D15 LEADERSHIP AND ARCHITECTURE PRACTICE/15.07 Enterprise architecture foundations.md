15.07 Enterprise architecture foundations

1 What is it

- Enterprise architecture (EA) is the practice of describing how a whole organization's business, data, applications and technology fit together, and then planning how to move from the current state to a target state.
- Think of it as a city plan. A solution architect designs one building. An enterprise architect decides the zones, roads and rules for the whole city.
- TOGAF (The Open Group Architecture Framework) is the most common EA method. ADM stands for Architecture Development Method, the step-by-step cycle inside TOGAF.
- Legacy equivalent: the architecture review board and the "technology standards" document in large .NET Framework shops, plus the long-range IT roadmap that lived in slides and Visio files.

2 Why needed

- Without EA, each project picks its own tools, duplicates systems and creates integrations nobody owns.
- With EA, the organization sees which capabilities exist, which systems support them, and where money is wasted.
- Without it, mergers, cloud migrations and compliance changes become guesswork.
- It links business goals (grow in a new market) to technology decisions (which platforms, which data).

3 Before you read

- Solution architecture: designing one system with its quality attributes.
- Business capability: something the business can do, such as "process claims" or "onboard customers".
- ADRs and C4 diagrams from topic 15.01.
- Trade-off analysis from topic 15.02.

4 Features and core concepts

- EA domains (business, data, application, technology)
  - Business: strategy, capabilities, processes, organization. Why it matters: technology must serve this layer.
  - Data: what data exists, who owns it, how it flows. Why it matters: most integration and compliance problems start here.
  - Application: the systems and services that deliver capabilities. Why it matters: shows overlap, such as three CRMs.
  - Technology: infrastructure, platforms, cloud, networks. Why it matters: sets the standards teams build on.
  - Memory aid: BDAT.

- TOGAF ADM phases
  - Preliminary: set up the architecture capability, principles and governance.
  - A, Architecture Vision: scope, stakeholders and the value of the change.
  - B, Business Architecture.
  - C, Information Systems Architecture (data and application).
  - D, Technology Architecture.
  - E, Opportunities and Solutions: group the work into projects.
  - F, Migration Planning: sequence and prioritize.
  - G, Implementation Governance: check that projects follow the architecture.
  - H, Architecture Change Management: handle changes and start the next cycle.
  - Requirements Management sits in the middle and connects all phases.
  - Why it matters: it is the standard exam answer for "what happens in which order".

- Architecture principles
  - Short rules such as "buy before build" or "data has one owner". Each has a statement, rationale and implications.
  - Why it matters: they settle repeated arguments without a meeting.

- Capability maps
  - A one-page map of what the business can do, shown as boxes, often colored by maturity or investment priority.
  - Why it matters: it lets leaders discuss priorities without talking about systems.

- Roadmaps
  - A time-ordered plan of transitions from current to target state.
  - Why it matters: it turns the vision into funded, sequenced work.

- Architecture repository
  - The store for models, standards, reference architectures, decisions and artifacts.
  - Why it matters: without it, knowledge sits in individual heads.

- Governance boards
  - A group that reviews designs for compliance with principles and approves exceptions (dispensations).
  - Why it matters: it keeps standards real, but a slow board becomes a bottleneck.

- ArchiMate awareness
  - An open modeling language for EA with layers for business, application and technology.
  - Why it matters: it gives a common notation. Know what it is, not every symbol.

- TOGAF Foundation and Practitioner syllabus (verify current version)
  - Foundation (Part 1) tests knowledge of terms and concepts. Practitioner (Part 2) tests applying them to scenarios. Check the Open Group site for the current edition, format and combined-exam options.

- Enterprise vs solution vs technical architect
  - Enterprise: whole-organization direction, standards and roadmaps.
  - Solution: end-to-end design of one business solution across systems.
  - Technical: deep design of a platform or technology area.
  - Why it matters: it decides which interview you are in and what answers fit.

5 Core focus (exam and interview)

- Name the ADM phases in order and say what each produces.
- Know that the ADM is iterative, with Requirements Management at the center.
- Know the four BDAT domains and the relationship between principles, standards and the repository.
- Know the difference between an architecture principle and a standard.
- Know what a dispensation is: an approved exception to the architecture.
- Practitioner-style scenarios: pick the right phase or the right artifact for a situation.
- Exam details such as question counts, pass marks and the current version change over time (verify on the Open Group site).

6 Real-world use case

- Situation: a Kolkata-based insurer has four policy systems after acquisitions, mostly .NET Framework, with duplicate customer data.
- Choice: the EA team builds a capability map, finds "policy servicing" supported by four systems, and sets a principle of one system of record per data domain. The roadmap consolidates onto two platforms, moving to Azure over three years using the strangler approach from 9.03.
- Trade-off: standardizing cuts cost and risk, but slows teams that need freedom and takes years to show payback.
- When NOT to use it: in a small company or a single product team, full TOGAF is overhead. A light version (principles, a capability map, ADRs) is enough.

7 Interview lens

- Q: What is the difference between an enterprise architect and a solution architect?
  - A: Enterprise architects set direction, principles and roadmaps across the whole organization. Solution architects design one solution within those rules.
- Q: How do you apply TOGAF without creating bureaucracy?
  - A: Tailor it. Use the phases as a checklist, produce only artifacts that drive decisions, and keep governance to short reviews.
- Q: How do you prove EA is worth the cost?
  - A: Tie it to outcomes: fewer duplicate systems, faster migration decisions, lower license and run cost, fewer audit findings.
- Q: What do you do when a project wants to break a principle?
  - A: Review the reason and impact. Approve a time-limited dispensation with a plan to return to the standard, or update the principle if it is outdated.

8 Common mistakes

- Treating the ADM as a strict waterfall. It shows up as months of documents before any value. Fix: iterate and deliver small.
- Ivory tower EA with no link to delivery. Shows up as standards that teams ignore. Fix: involve delivery teams and measure adoption.
- Too many artifacts. The repository fills with unread diagrams. Fix: produce only what supports a decision.
- Governance as a gate that blocks. Projects wait weeks. Fix: guardrails, self-service checks, and fast reviews for low-risk work.
- Confusing a principle with a technology choice. Fix: principles state intent, standards state products.

9 Mental model

- City planning:
  - Vision and principles = city goals and zoning rules
  - BDAT domains = people and services, records, buildings, roads and utilities
  - Roadmap = the phased construction plan
  - Governance = building permits
- The ADM loop: Vision, then Business, Information Systems, Technology, then Opportunities, Migration, Governance, Change, and back to Vision.

10 Mini lab

- Time: about 40 minutes. Tools: pen and paper or a free diagram tool such as draw.io or Mermaid. No Azure needed.
- Steps:
  - Pick your current or a past .NET project.
  - Write three architecture principles, each with statement, rationale and implication.
  - Draw a one-page capability map with six to ten business capabilities.
  - Under each capability, list the application that supports it and the hosting technology. Mark overlaps.
  - Write a three-step roadmap (now, next, later) for removing one overlap.
  - Map each step to an ADM phase name.
- Expected result: one page showing capabilities, supporting systems, overlaps and a sequenced roadmap, plus three principles.
- Clean-up: save the page in your capstone repository docs folder, or discard it.

11 Revision summary

- EA has four domains: business, data, application, technology (BDAT).
- ADM order: Preliminary, A Vision, B Business, C Information Systems, D Technology, E Opportunities and Solutions, F Migration Planning, G Implementation Governance, H Change Management.
- Requirements Management is at the center of the ADM and applies to every phase.
- A principle states intent; a standard states the specific technology or rule.
- A dispensation is an approved, usually time-limited, exception to the architecture.
- A capability map shows what the business can do, not which systems exist.
- ArchiMate is a modeling language; TOGAF is a method.
- Enterprise architects set direction, solution architects design one solution, technical architects go deep on a platform.
- Check the current TOGAF exam version and format on the Open Group site (verify).
