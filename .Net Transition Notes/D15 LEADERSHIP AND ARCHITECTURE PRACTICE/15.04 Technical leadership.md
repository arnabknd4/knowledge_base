1 What is it
- A software project's work is more than writing code. Technical leadership means planning, estimating, managing risk, handing work to others, growing people and dealing with clients.
- Estimation is a guess about effort. You give a range, not a promise.
- Scope is what is included in the work. Negotiating scope means agreeing what to cut or delay when time is short.
- Legacy equivalent: the tech lead role in a .NET Framework project: sprint planning, allocating work, reviewing pull requests, reporting status to the client manager.

2 Why needed
- Good code from a team still fails if estimates are wrong, risks are hidden or work is not spread well.
- Without leadership habits: missed deadlines, burnt-out seniors, juniors who never grow, tech debt that piles up, and clients who lose trust.
- Architects are judged on how well a team delivers, not only on designs.

3 Before you read
- Scrum basics: sprint, backlog, story.
- Experience of at least one delivery cycle, for the stories you will recall.
- Basic idea of what a pull request review is.

4 Features and core concepts
- Estimation: predicting effort before work is done. It matters because plans, cost and promises depend on it.
  - Story points: a relative size unit (bigger number, more effort, risk and unknowns). Not hours. Compare a new story to one you already finished.
  - T-shirt sizes: S, M, L, XL for early, rough sizing when details are missing. Matters for roadmaps and early client talks.
  - Three-point estimate: give optimistic (O), most likely (M) and pessimistic (P). Expected = (O + 4M + P) / 6. It shows uncertainty in numbers.
- Planning and scope negotiation: fitting work into time and capacity. Matters because the client always wants more. Offer options: cut scope, move date, add people (with a warning that this has limits), or reduce quality (usually refuse).
- Risk management: listing what could go wrong, then rating likelihood and impact. For each risk: avoid, reduce, transfer or accept. Matters because surprises are cheaper to handle early. Keep a risk register with owner and next review date.
- Delegation: handing over work with clear outcome, limits and check-in points. Matters because a lead who does everything becomes the bottleneck. Delegate the outcome, not the exact method.
- Mentoring and growing leads: coaching people to take bigger responsibility. Matters because your team's ceiling is your ceiling. Use pairing, stretch tasks, regular one-to-ones, honest feedback.
- Code review culture: reviews are for shared learning and quality, not blame. Matters because tone decides whether people hide mistakes. Keep pull requests small, comment on the code not the person, automate style checks so humans discuss design.
- Onboarding: getting a new member productive. Matters because slow onboarding wastes weeks of salary. Use a written setup guide, a starter task, a buddy.
- Technical roadmap: a time-ordered plan of technical goals (upgrades, migrations, platform work) linked to business goals. Matters because technical work needs a visible place next to features.
- Tech debt budget: reserving a fixed share of each sprint (often 10 to 20 percent, adjust to your team) for cleanup. Matters because debt left alone slows every later change.
- Client stakeholder management: knowing who the client people are, what each wants and how often to update them. Matters because surprises damage trust faster than bad news does. Give bad news early, with options.
- Offshore team coordination: working across time zones. Matters in Indian delivery models with onsite and offshore splits. Use written handover notes, a fixed overlap window, clear definition of done, recorded decisions.
- Worth memorizing: Expected = (O + 4M + P) / 6

5 Core focus (exam and interview)
- Most asked: how you estimate, what you do when you are late, how you handle a missed estimate, how you say no to a client.
- Know the difference between estimate, target and commitment.
- Know story points are relative and velocity is the points a team finishes per sprint, used to forecast, not to compare teams.
- Know three-point and why ranges beat single numbers.
- Expect the answer shape: situation, action, number, result.
- Expect scope triangle talk: scope, time, cost, with quality as a protected fourth.

6 Real-world use case
- Situation: a client asks to add a reporting module to a .NET and Azure project, two weeks before release, with no extra people.
- Choice made: the lead gives a three-point estimate (5, 8, 15 days, expected about 8.7), lists risks (unclear report fields, SQL load), and offers three options: release without it, release it in a second drop, or move the date. The client picks the second drop.
- Trade-off: honest ranges can sound weak to a client who wants one number. You gain trust, you lose a quick yes.
- When NOT to use it: for a tiny, well-known task, a heavy estimate session wastes time. A quick T-shirt size is enough.

7 Interview lens
- Q: How do you estimate when requirements are unclear?
  A: I give a range with stated assumptions and a T-shirt size first. I refine with a short spike (a time-boxed experiment) and re-estimate.
- Q: The client wants more scope with the same date. What do you do?
  A: I show the cost of each option: cut something, move the date, or phase delivery. I let the client choose with the facts in front of them.
- Q: How do you grow a senior developer into a lead?
  A: I hand over a real outcome such as running a sprint or a design, agree check-ins, and give feedback within days. I step back slowly.
- Q: How do you keep tech debt from being ignored?
  A: I keep a visible debt list, reserve a fixed share of each sprint, and explain each item in business terms such as slower releases or incidents.

8 Common mistakes
- Giving one exact date from a guess. Shows up as missed deadline and lost trust. Fix: give ranges and assumptions.
- Using velocity to rank developers or teams. Shows up as inflated points. Fix: use it only for forecasting.
- Doing the hard work yourself instead of delegating. Shows up as you being the bottleneck and the team not growing. Fix: delegate outcomes, keep check-ins.
- Hiding bad news. Shows up as a bigger crisis later. Fix: report early with options.
- Harsh or vague code reviews. Shows up as hidden problems and quiet people. Fix: specific, kind, small reviews.
- No debt budget. Shows up as every sprint getting slower. Fix: fixed share per sprint.
- No written handover for offshore. Shows up as one day lost per question. Fix: written notes and an overlap window.

9 Mental model
- Lead = a manager of uncertainty, people and promises.

  Estimate (range) -> Plan (scope vs date) -> Risks (owner) -> Delegate -> Review -> Report (early, with options)
        ^                                                                                   |
        +--------------------------- learn and re-estimate <-------------------------------+

10 Mini lab
- Time: 30 minutes. Tools: pen and paper or a spreadsheet. No cloud needed.
- Steps:
  - Pick one real task from your current project, such as "add an export to Excel".
  - Write O, M and P in days. Example: 2, 4, 9.
  - Compute (O + 4M + P) / 6. For 2, 4, 9 you should get 4.5 days.
  - Write three assumptions that would change the number.
  - Create a risk register with three rows: risk, likelihood (low, medium, high), impact, response, owner.
  - Write one scope option you would offer if the client wants it in half the time.
  - Practice saying the estimate and options aloud in two minutes, as if to a client.
- Expected result: one range with assumptions, three rated risks, and a spoken answer without the word "maybe".
- Clean-up: file the page with your story bank notes. Nothing to delete.

11 Revision summary
- Estimate is a range, not a promise.
- Expected = (O + 4M + P) / 6.
- Story points are relative; velocity forecasts, never ranks people.
- Scope negotiation = offer options: cut, move, phase.
- Risk register: risk, rating, response, owner, review date.
- Delegate outcomes, not methods.
- Reserve a fixed share of each sprint for tech debt.
- Report bad news early with options; offshore needs written handovers.
