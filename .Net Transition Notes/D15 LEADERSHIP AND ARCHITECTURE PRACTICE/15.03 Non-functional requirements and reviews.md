1 What is it
- A review and governance practice for non-functional requirements (NFRs): the qualities a system must have beyond its features.
- NFRs are speed, uptime, security, growth capacity, ease of change, visibility into behaviour, legal fit and cost.
- This topic covers how to write them as measurable targets, check a design against them, and govern them across teams.
- Legacy equivalent: the "non-functional" section of a requirements document and the go-live checklist in .NET Framework projects, usually written late and checked by nobody.

2 Why needed
- Features can pass every test and still fail in production because the site is slow, down, insecure or too costly.
- Vague NFRs ("should be fast") cannot be tested, so they get missed.
- Without a review step, each team makes its own choices and the architecture drifts.
- Without governance, either nobody checks anything or a central board blocks every release.

4 Features and core concepts
- NFR catalog: a standard list of quality areas to cover so nothing is forgotten. Why it matters: it works as a prompt for every new project.
  - Performance: how fast and how much load. Example target: p95 latency under 300 ms at 500 requests per second.
  - Availability: share of time the system works. Example target: 99.9 percent monthly.
  - Security: protection of data and access. Example target: all data encrypted, no secrets in code.
  - Scalability: handling growth. Example target: 3x load with only configuration change.
  - Maintainability: ease of change. Example target: new developer ships a fix in the first week.
  - Observability: ability to see what the system is doing. Example target: every request traceable end to end.
  - Compliance: legal and audit needs. Example target: data stays in India region, audit log kept 1 year.
  - Cost: running expense. Example target: under a set monthly budget per environment.
- Measurable targets: each NFR gets a number, a measuring method and a condition. Why it matters: a target you cannot measure cannot be tested or alerted on. Pattern: "Under [condition], [measure] is [number], measured by [tool]."
- Architecture review checklist: a fixed list of questions asked of every design. Why it matters: catches gaps early and gives consistent reviews.
- Design review process: a meeting where the design is walked through before building. Why it matters: cheap place to find expensive mistakes. Keep it short, with the design doc sent in advance.
- Production readiness review (PRR): a check before go-live that monitoring, alerts, backups, runbooks, scaling and rollback exist. Why it matters: stops launching something that nobody can support.
- Architecture governance: the rules for who decides what and how standards are enforced. Why it matters: keeps many teams aligned without slowing them.
- Technology radar: a list of technologies marked adopt, trial, assess or hold. Why it matters: gives teams a clear, shared view of approved choices.
- Guardrails vs control: guardrails are automatic limits that teams work inside (policies, templates, pipeline checks). Control means manual approval for each change. Why it matters: guardrails scale, control creates queues.
- Worth memorizing: write each NFR as "condition, measure, number, tool".

5 Core focus (exam and interview)
- Most asked: how to turn "must be fast and reliable" into testable numbers.
- Most asked: what goes in a review checklist, and what a PRR checks before go-live.
- Know that NFRs conflict: high availability raises cost, strong security can add latency. Decisions must be recorded as trade-offs.
- Know the Azure link: the Well-Architected Framework pillars map closely to the NFR catalog (verify current pillar names).
- Remember the rule: guardrails over gates. Automate checks in pipelines and Azure Policy where possible.
- Remember: NFRs are gathered at the start, checked at design, tested before release, and monitored after.

6 Real-world use case
- Situation: a client in Kolkata is moving a legacy MVC order system to Azure App Service and Azure SQL. The old requirements said only "must be fast".
- Choice made: write eight NFRs with numbers (for example checkout p95 under 500 ms, 99.9 percent availability, RPO 15 minutes, RTO 1 hour, data in India region). Add them to the design doc. Use a short checklist in design review. Run a PRR one week before go-live. Add policy checks in the pipeline and Azure Policy for region and tagging.
- Trade-off: the targets forced a zone-redundant database and a Redis cache, which raised monthly cost. The client accepted this after seeing the cost beside the availability gain.
- When NOT to use it: for a small internal tool or short prototype, a full catalog, review board and PRR is too heavy. Use three or four key NFRs and a one-page check. Also avoid a central board approving every change.

7 Interview lens
- Q: How do you make an NFR testable?
  A: State the condition, the measure, the target number and the tool that measures it. Then add it to load tests and alerts.
- Q: What is a production readiness review?
  A: A pre-launch check that monitoring, alerts, backups, runbooks, scaling and rollback are in place. It confirms the team can operate the system, not just build it.
- Q: How do you govern architecture without slowing teams?
  A: Use guardrails such as templates, pipeline checks and Azure Policy, and keep human review for high-impact decisions only. Record decisions in ADRs.
- Q: What do you do when two NFRs conflict?
  A: Rank them with the stakeholders, show the cost and risk of each option, and record the decision and reasons in an ADR. Revisit it when conditions change.

8 Common mistakes
- Vague targets like "fast" or "secure". Shows up as arguments after go-live. Fix: numbers plus measuring method.
- NFRs written once and forgotten. Shows up as drift and surprise outages. Fix: turn them into tests, alerts and dashboards.
- Review board as a gate for everything. Shows up as long waits and teams going around it. Fix: guardrails, and review only high-impact changes.
- Same targets for every component. Shows up as overspending. Fix: set targets by business importance.
- PRR skipped under deadline pressure. Shows up as launch with no alerts or runbook. Fix: make PRR a required pipeline or release step.
- Ignoring cost as an NFR. Shows up as a large bill. Fix: set budgets and alerts from the start.

9 Mental model
Requirement gathering sets NFR numbers, then design review checks them, then the build and tests prove them, then PRR approves launch, then monitoring keeps checking.

  NFR catalog -> measurable targets -> design review -> tests -> PRR -> monitor
                       ^                                                |
                       +------------------ feedback, ADRs --------------+

  Guardrails (policy, templates, pipeline checks) run along the whole path.

10 Mini lab
- Time: about 30 minutes, paper or a text file, no Azure cost.
- Pick one app you know well (for example an order system you worked on).
- Step 1: list the eight catalog areas.
- Step 2: for each, write one NFR in the form "Under [condition], [measure] is [number], measured by [tool]".
- Step 3: mark which ones you could test today and which have no measuring tool.
- Step 4: write a 10-question review checklist from the gaps you found.
- Step 5: pick one NFR (for example availability) and write the alert rule or load test that would prove it.
- Expected result: eight measurable NFRs, a gap list, a 10-question checklist and one test or alert design.
- Clean-up: save the file in your capstone project folder. Nothing to delete.

11 Revision summary
- NFR catalog areas: performance, availability, security, scalability, maintainability, observability, compliance, cost.
- Good NFR pattern: condition, measure, number, tool.
- NFRs conflict, so rank them and record the decision in an ADR.
- Review checklist and design review catch gaps before building.
- PRR checks monitoring, alerts, backups, runbooks, scaling and rollback before go-live.
- Technology radar uses adopt, trial, assess, hold.
- Guardrails scale; manual control creates queues.
- Skip heavy governance for small tools and prototypes.
