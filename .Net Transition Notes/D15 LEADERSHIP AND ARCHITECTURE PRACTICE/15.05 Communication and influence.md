1 What is it
- Influence without a title means getting people to act on your technical ideas when you are not their manager. It rests on trust, clear writing and good timing.
- Communication and influence for a lead covers explaining, writing, presenting, handling pushback and reaching agreement across technical and non-technical people.
- Legacy equivalent: the status mails, Word design documents and review meetings you already ran, now done deliberately with a structure.

2 Why needed
- Architects do not write most of the code. Their output is decisions that others accept and carry out.
- A good design that nobody understands or trusts does not get built. A weaker design that is explained well often wins.
- Without these skills you get unread documents, stalled approvals, repeated meetings, surprise conflicts and a reputation as someone who is "technical but hard to work with".
- Client and management roles in the Kolkata and remote market test this in almost every senior interview.

3 Before you read
- Know the difference between a decision (what we chose) and a design (how it works).
- Know your audience types: developers, delivery manager, client, executive.
- Know what an ADR is (topic 15.01).
- Know the basic quality attributes: cost, risk, time, performance, security.

4 Features and core concepts
- Explaining to non-technical audiences: say what it does for the business first, then one level of how. It matters because managers decide on outcomes, not on technology. Use analogies from their world, and drop acronyms.
- Writing proposals and RFCs: a short document stating the problem, options, recommendation and ask. It matters because a written proposal can be reviewed, commented on and kept, while a meeting is forgotten.
- Executive summaries: five lines at the top giving the decision needed, the recommendation, the cost, the risk and the deadline. It matters because senior people read only the top.
- Cost and risk framing: state the cost of doing it, the cost of not doing it, and what could go wrong with a rollback option. It matters because leaders compare money and risk, not elegance.
- Presenting designs: lead with context and constraints, show one diagram, show the options with trade-offs, then your recommendation. It matters because a design shown without constraints invites "why not X?" attacks.
- Handling objections: listen fully, restate the concern, separate the fact from the fear, answer with evidence, and offer a test or pilot. It matters because most objections are about risk or workload, not about the technology.
- Building consensus: talk to key people one to one before the meeting, collect concerns early and adjust the proposal. It matters because meetings should confirm agreement, not create it.
- Influencing without authority: build credibility with small delivered wins, help others first, use data and prototypes, and make the decision easy for them. It matters because architects and leads rarely control the people who must implement.
- Conflict resolution: separate the person from the problem, find the shared goal, agree on the decision criteria, then decide. Escalate only after trying direct talk. It matters because unresolved technical disputes become delivery delays.
- Status reporting: progress against plan, next steps, risks with owners, and decisions needed. Keep one format every week. It matters because predictable reports build trust and surface problems early.
- Decision logs: a list with date, decision, reason, owner and who was consulted. It matters because teams forget why choices were made and reopen them.
- Memorize this proposal skeleton: Problem, Goals and constraints, Options, Recommendation, Cost and risk, Ask and deadline.

5 Core focus (exam and interview)
- Most asked: "Tell me about a time you convinced someone." "How do you explain a technical issue to a client?" "How do you handle a disagreement with another lead?"
- Answer with STAR: situation, task, action, result, with a number in the result.
- Interviewers look for: you listened first, you used data, you found a compromise and the outcome was measurable.
- Defaults to remember: write the ask in the first paragraph, keep proposals to two pages, show two or three options (never one), always include a "do nothing" option.
- Not asked in exams like AZ-305, but this is central in architect and client-facing rounds.

6 Real-world use case
- Situation: a client project runs on an IIS-hosted monolith. You want to introduce a message queue for order processing. The client manager worries about cost and a delivery delay. A senior developer fears extra complexity.
- Choice: you write a two-page RFC with three options (do nothing, queue, full microservices). You meet the manager and the developer separately first. You propose a four-week pilot on one flow with a rollback plan and a cost estimate for Azure Service Bus.
- Result: the manager approves because risk and cost are bounded. The developer joins the pilot and owns the design review.
- Trade-off: pre-meetings and writing cost time up front, and the process is slower than just deciding.
- When NOT to use it: urgent production incidents, or small reversible changes. Decide, tell people, and move on. Heavy process for small choices makes you look slow.

7 Interview lens
- Q: How do you explain a technical risk to a non-technical manager?
  A: State the business impact, the likelihood and the cost to fix now versus later. Skip internal details unless asked.
- Q: How do you influence a team that does not report to you?
  A: Build trust through small wins and help, share data or a prototype, and involve them in the design. Make the right choice the easy one.
- Q: What do you do when two senior engineers disagree on a design?
  A: Agree on the criteria first (cost, risk, time, team skill), compare the options against them, and run a small test if still unclear. Record the outcome in an ADR.
- Q: What goes in a good status report?
  A: Progress against plan, next steps, top risks with owners, and decisions needed. Same format every time, short enough to read in two minutes.

8 Common mistakes
- Starting with technology instead of the problem. It shows up as blank faces in the meeting. Fix: open with the business impact.
- Long documents with the ask hidden at the end. It shows up as "I did not get to read it". Fix: executive summary at the top.
- Surprising people in a meeting. It shows up as defensive pushback. Fix: pre-meetings and early drafts.
- Presenting only one option. It shows up as "why not something else?" Fix: show two or three options with trade-offs.
- Arguing to win. It shows up as damaged relationships and quiet non-cooperation. Fix: aim for the shared goal, and concede small points.
- Verbal decisions only. It shows up as the same debate three months later. Fix: decision log or ADR.
- Hiding bad news in status reports. It shows up as late escalation and lost trust. Fix: report risks early with a plan.

9 Mental model
- Think of it as a funnel from idea to action:

  Problem -> Audience -> Options with trade-offs -> Pre-talks -> Written proposal -> Meeting to confirm -> Decision log -> Follow-up report

- Trust is the fuel. Each delivered promise adds fuel, each surprise removes it.

10 Mini lab
- Time: about 40 minutes. Tools: any text editor.
- Steps:
  - Pick a real or imagined change, for example moving report generation to a background queue.
  - Write a one-page RFC using the skeleton: Problem, Goals and constraints, Options (three, including do nothing), Recommendation, Cost and risk, Ask and deadline.
  - Write a five-line executive summary on top.
  - Write the same recommendation in three sentences for a non-technical manager, with no acronyms.
  - List the top three objections you expect, with one answer each.
  - Add one decision log row: date, decision, reason, owner, consulted.
- Expected result: a one-page RFC, a five-line summary, a three-sentence manager version, three prepared answers and one log entry. Keep the file as a template for the capstone project.
- Clean-up: none. Save the RFC in the capstone repository under a docs folder.

11 Revision summary
- Lead with the business problem, then the technical detail.
- Proposal skeleton: Problem, Goals, Options, Recommendation, Cost and risk, Ask.
- Always show two or three options, including do nothing.
- Executive summary: decision needed, recommendation, cost, risk, deadline.
- Pre-meet key people one to one, so the meeting only confirms.
- Handle objections by listening, restating, answering with evidence and offering a pilot.
- Influence without authority comes from small wins, data and making the decision easy.
- Write decisions in a log or ADR to stop repeat debates.
