1 What is it
- Trade-off analysis is the habit of comparing design options against measurable quality goals, then choosing one and recording why.
- Instead of "I prefer X", you say "X wins on availability and cost, loses on time to build, and the team can run it".
- Tools: quality attribute scenarios, utility trees, ATAM awareness, weighted decision matrices, build vs buy, reversible vs irreversible decisions, risk register, spike and proof-of-concept, fitness functions, evolutionary architecture.
- Legacy equivalent: the "which technology do we use" meeting in a .NET Framework project, usually settled by seniority or habit, with no written criteria.

2 Why needed
- Every design choice improves one quality and hurts another. Caching speeds reads but risks stale data.
- Without a structured comparison, decisions follow opinion, fashion or the loudest person.
- Unrecorded reasons mean nobody can tell later whether a decision still holds.
- Architect interviews test this directly: "why this and not that".

3 Before you read
- Quality attributes: availability, latency, security, cost, operability (topic 11.01).
- ADRs: a short record of a decision and its reasons (topic 15.01).
- SLI and SLO: a measured number and the target set for it.
- What a monolith and a microservice are, from your own projects.

4 Features and core concepts
- Quality attribute scenario: one testable sentence in six parts: source, stimulus, artifact, environment, response, measure. Example: "At peak load, 5000 users place orders, and the API responds under 300 ms at p95." It matters because "make it fast" cannot be tested.
- Utility tree: a tree with quality goals at the top, refined into scenarios, each ranked by business importance and technical difficulty. It matters because it shows which scenarios decide the design.
- ATAM awareness: Architecture Tradeoff Analysis Method. A structured review where stakeholders list scenarios and the team finds risks, sensitivity points and trade-off points. Know the idea and its output, not the full procedure.
- Weighted decision matrix: options in columns, criteria in rows, weights per criterion, scores per option, total at the bottom. It forces criteria to be agreed before scoring. The weights are opinion, so show them to stakeholders.
- Cost, risk, time and team skill: the four constraints beside the technical fit. A perfect design the team cannot run is a bad design.
- Build vs buy: build when it is your differentiator, buy when it is generic (identity, payments, email). Count the total cost of ownership, including upgrades and exit cost.
- Reversible vs irreversible decisions: a two-way door can be changed cheaply, so decide fast. A one-way door (database engine, cloud lock-in, public API contract) deserves more analysis. It matters because it sets how much analysis a decision deserves.
- Risk register: a list of risks with likelihood, impact, owner and mitigation. It matters because accepted trade-offs become tracked risks.
- Spike and proof-of-concept: a short timeboxed experiment to answer one question, for example "can Service Bus handle our message size". Write the question first and keep the result, not the code.
- Fitness functions: an automated check that a quality goal still holds, for example a NetArchTest rule or a load test threshold in the pipeline. It matters because it stops the design drifting.
- Evolutionary architecture: design so that decisions can change later, guarded by fitness functions, rather than predicting everything now.
- Worth memorizing: scenario template. "When [stimulus] happens to [artifact] in [environment], the system [response] within [measure]."

5 Core focus (exam and interview)
- Most asked: "How do you choose between two options?" Answer with criteria, weights, evidence from a spike, a recorded ADR and a revisit trigger.
- Know the difference between a sensitivity point (one choice strongly affects one quality) and a trade-off point (one choice affects several qualities in opposite directions).
- Always state what you gave up, not only what you gained.
- Azure Well-Architected pillars (reliability, security, cost optimization, operational excellence, performance efficiency) are the standard vocabulary for trade-offs in AZ-305 style questions. (verify current pillar names)
- No hard limits to memorize. Memorize the structure of a scenario and the matrix.

6 Real-world use case
- Situation: an order system in a legacy .NET Framework monolith must handle 10x festival-season traffic. Options: scale up App Service, add Redis caching, or split order processing into a Service Bus worker.
- Scenario: "At 10x load, order placement succeeds for 99.9 percent of requests with p95 under 500 ms."
- Choice: Redis cache for catalogue reads plus a queue and worker for order confirmation. A 1-week spike on the queue showed the throughput was enough.
- Trade-off: eventual consistency in order status, a new component to run and monitor, and the team needs to learn idempotent consumers. It was recorded in an ADR and in the risk register.
- When NOT to use the heavy method: small, reversible choices such as a logging library. Use a short ADR or a quick decision, not a full matrix.

7 Interview lens
- Q: How do you decide between two architectures?
  A: I write quality scenarios, weight the criteria with stakeholders, score the options, and test the riskiest assumption with a spike. Then I record the result in an ADR.
- Q: What is the difference between a sensitivity point and a trade-off point?
  A: A sensitivity point is a choice that strongly affects one quality. A trade-off point affects two or more qualities in opposite directions, for example encryption helping security but hurting latency.
- Q: When do you build and when do you buy?
  A: I build what differentiates the business and buy what is generic. I compare total cost, including maintenance and exit cost, not only licence price.
- Q: How do you stop an architecture from drifting after the decision?
  A: I turn the key goals into fitness functions, such as architecture tests and load-test thresholds in the pipeline. I also revisit ADRs when a trigger condition is hit.

8 Common mistakes
- Scoring a matrix after the favourite is already chosen. It shows up as weights tuned to give the preferred answer. Fix: agree criteria and weights first.
- Vague goals such as "must be scalable". It shows up as arguments in review. Fix: write measurable scenarios.
- Over-analysing two-way-door decisions. It shows up as weeks lost on small choices. Fix: classify reversibility first.
- A spike that turns into production code. It shows up as shortcuts and no tests. Fix: write the question first and discard the code.
- Not recording the rejected options. It shows up when the same debate returns in a year. Fix: keep options and consequences in the ADR.
- Ignoring team skill. It shows up as a design nobody can operate at 2 am. Fix: score team skill as a criterion.

9 Mental model
- A balance scale in a shop: quality goals are the weights, options are the goods, and the ADR is the receipt.

  Goals (measured) -> Options -> Score and test -> Decide -> Record (ADR) -> Guard (fitness function) -> Revisit when the trigger fires

10 Mini lab (about 30 minutes, paper and local tools)
- Step 1: pick a real decision from your work, for example Redis cache vs in-memory cache for a product catalogue.
- Step 2: write 3 quality scenarios in the six-part form, each with a number.
- Step 3: make a matrix with 5 criteria (latency, consistency, cost, operability, team skill). Give weights that add up to 100.
- Step 4: score both options 1 to 5 and calculate the weighted totals.
- Step 5: write one question for a spike, for example "does a distributed cache cut p95 below 200 ms". Run a 10-minute test locally with a small console app or k6.
- Step 6: write a 10-line ADR with context, options, decision, consequences and status.
- Expected result: one matrix, one ADR, and one number from the spike.
- Clean-up: delete the test app. Keep the matrix and ADR in your notes folder.

11 Revision summary
- A quality scenario has six parts and ends with a measurable response.
- A utility tree ranks scenarios by business value and difficulty.
- ATAM finds risks, sensitivity points and trade-off points.
- Weighted matrix: agree criteria and weights before scoring.
- Constraints to count: cost, risk, time, team skill.
- Reversible choices: decide fast. Irreversible choices: analyse more.
- A spike answers one question and its code is thrown away.
- A fitness function is an automated check that guards a quality goal.
