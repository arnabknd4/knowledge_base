1 What is it
- The Azure Well-Architected Framework (WAF) is Microsoft's set of design guidance for judging and improving a cloud workload against five pillars: reliability, security, cost optimization, operational excellence, performance efficiency.
- It gives a shared vocabulary, a review process and checklists, so architecture decisions are explained as trade-offs between pillars instead of personal opinion.
- It sits beside the Azure Architecture Center, which holds reference architectures and architecture style guides.
- Legacy equivalent: the non-functional requirements section of a design document, plus the architecture review checklist you filled in before go-live. WAF turns that into a standard, reusable method.

2 Why needed
- Teams optimise what they can see. Features and speed get attention, while resilience, cost and operability are discovered in production.
- Without a common framework, reviews depend on who is in the room, and the same gaps repeat across projects.
- It makes trade-offs explicit. Higher reliability costs money, and tighter security can slow delivery.
- It gives you a defensible structure for architect interviews and for AZ-305 case studies.

3 Before you read
- Know what a region, availability zone and paired region are (12.01).
- Know the difference between SLA, SLO, RPO and RTO.
- Know the basic Azure services: App Service, SQL Database, Storage, Front Door.
- Know what a non-functional requirement is (latency, availability, security targets).

4 Features and core concepts

Five pillars and their trade-offs
- Reliability: the workload keeps working and recovers from failure. Why: downtime costs money and trust.
- Security: protect data and systems, using least privilege, defence in depth and assume breach. Why: breaches are costly and hard to undo.
- Cost optimization: spend only for value delivered. Why: cloud bills grow silently.
- Operational excellence: deploy, monitor and run safely and repeatably. Why: most outages come from change and weak operations.
- Performance efficiency: meet demand with the right resources as load changes. Why: users leave slow systems.
- Typical trade-offs: more reliability (multi-region) raises cost. More security controls add latency and operational work. Aggressive cost cutting reduces headroom and resilience.
- Pillar order is not fixed. The business decides which pillars lead for each workload.

Workload assessment
- A structured review of one workload against the pillars, using the Azure Well-Architected Review and the Azure Advisor recommendations.
- Plain meaning: score the current design, find the risks, then rank fixes by impact and effort.
- Why it matters: it produces a prioritised backlog and an evidence trail for stakeholders.

Reliability patterns (zones, regions)
- Zones protect against a datacenter failure inside one region. Regions protect against a whole region failure.
- Zone-redundant services spread copies across zones automatically. Multi-region needs traffic routing (Front Door, Traffic Manager) and data replication.
- Why it matters: you pick the lowest level of redundancy that meets the RTO, RPO and SLA target.
- Composite SLA: multiply the SLAs of serial dependencies. Redundant parallel paths raise the combined figure.

Cost optimization
- Right-size resources, use reservations and savings plans for steady load, autoscale, and shut down dev and test environments.
- Why it matters: cost is a design property, not an afterthought. Use tags to allocate spend.

Operational excellence
- Infrastructure as code, CI/CD, safe deployment, monitoring, alerts, runbooks and incident reviews.
- Why it matters: repeatable operations reduce human error, which is a leading cause of outages.

Performance efficiency
- Scale out when possible, use caching, a CDN and async processing, and test with load tests to set a baseline.
- Why it matters: you size from measured data, not guesses.

Reference architectures (Azure Architecture Center)
- Tested diagrams and guidance for common solutions, such as web app with SQL, or microservices on AKS.
- Why it matters: start from a proven design and adapt it, rather than inventing.

Architecture styles
- N-tier: layers such as web, business and data on separate tiers. Familiar for legacy migrations.
- Web-queue-worker: a web front end, a queue and a background worker. Good for long-running work.
- Microservices: small independently deployed services. Needs strong DevOps and observability.
- Event-driven: producers and consumers linked by events through a broker. Good for decoupling and scale.
- Why it matters: style choice drives which pillars are easy or hard.

One command worth remembering
- az advisor recommendation list --category Cost (categories also include HighAvailability, Security, OperationalExcellence, Performance) (verify)

5 Core focus (exam and interview)
- Most asked: name the five pillars and give a trade-off between two of them for a scenario.
- Match requirement to pattern: need to survive datacenter loss means zones. Need to survive region loss means multi-region.
- Composite SLA math: serial services multiply, so the result is lower than the weakest service.
- Choose the architecture style from team skill, scale and coupling needs. Microservices is not the default.
- Know the tools: Azure Well-Architected Review, Advisor, Architecture Center, Azure Pricing Calculator.
- The pillar names and review tooling change over time (verify on Microsoft Learn).
- Exam trap: picking the most resilient option when the stated priority is cost.

6 Real-world use case
- Situation: a legacy .NET Framework order portal is moved to Azure for a retail client. It must handle a festival-season peak and the business wants 99.9 percent availability on a tight budget.
- Choice made: web-queue-worker style. App Service in a zone-redundant plan for the web tier, Azure SQL with zone redundancy, Service Bus plus a worker for order processing, Front Door for routing and WAF, Application Insights for monitoring. Autoscale for the peak and reservations for the baseline.
- Trade-off: zone redundancy and Service Bus add cost, but multi-region was rejected. Instead the design relies on geo-backups and a documented restore runbook, accepting a longer RTO.
- Decision recorded as an ADR with the pillar trade-off written down.
- When NOT to use heavy WAF process: a throwaway prototype or a small internal tool. A light checklist is enough. Also do not over-engineer multi-region when the business does not require it.

7 Interview lens
- Q: What are the five pillars?
 A: Reliability, security, cost optimization, operational excellence, performance efficiency. Each is a lens for judging a workload, not a separate project.
- Q: How do you handle conflicting pillars?
 A: Ask the business which outcome matters most for this workload, then record the choice and its cost in an ADR. Review it when requirements change.
- Q: Zones or regions for resilience?
 A: Zones for datacenter-level failure at low cost and latency. Regions for regional outage or compliance, with higher cost and replication complexity.
- Q: How would you review an existing workload?
 A: Run the Well-Architected Review, check Advisor, map findings to pillars, rank by risk and effort, and turn the top items into backlog work with owners.

8 Common mistakes
- Treating WAF as a one-time audit. Shows up as drift and repeated incidents. Fix: schedule reviews and tie findings to the backlog.
- Maximising every pillar. Shows up as a high bill and slow delivery. Fix: rank pillars per workload.
- Assuming zones equal disaster recovery. Shows up when a region fails and recovery is slow. Fix: define RPO and RTO and test a restore.
- Ignoring operational excellence. Shows up as manual deployments and long outages. Fix: IaC, pipelines and runbooks.
- Choosing microservices by fashion. Shows up as a distributed monolith. Fix: choose the style from team size and need.
- Cost decisions without tags or budgets. Shows up as unexplained bills. Fix: tag, set budgets and alerts.

10 Mini lab (30 minutes)
- Needs: a free Azure account and the Azure portal.
- Step 1: Create a resource group named rg-waf-lab and deploy one App Service (free or B1 plan) and one Azure SQL database (basic or serverless free offer, verify availability).
- Step 2: Open Azure Advisor and read the recommendations for Reliability, Security, Cost, Operational excellence and Performance. Note two per pillar if present. New resources may show few items.
- Step 3: Open the Azure Well-Architected Review assessment from Microsoft Learn and answer the reliability questions for this small workload (verify current location).
- Step 4: On paper, write one trade-off: for example, adding zone redundancy to App Service and SQL, what you gain and what it adds to cost.
- Expected result: a short list of recommendations mapped to pillars and one written trade-off.
- Clean-up: delete the resource group with az group delete --name rg-waf-lab --yes

11 Revision summary
- Five pillars: reliability, security, cost optimization, operational excellence, performance efficiency.
- Pillars conflict, so the business priority decides and an ADR records it.
- Zones cover datacenter failure. Regions cover regional failure.
- Serial dependencies multiply SLAs, so the composite is lower than the weakest.
- Tools: Well-Architected Review, Advisor, Architecture Center, Pricing Calculator.
- Styles: N-tier, web-queue-worker, microservices, event-driven. Choose by team and need.
- WAF is a repeating review, not a one-time audit.
