1 What is it

An Azure landing zone is a planned foundation for running applications and services in Microsoft Azure. It defines how subscriptions, networking, identity, security, governance, and shared services fit together before teams deploy workloads.

A landing zone is not just a virtual network or a single subscription. It is an architecture that gives teams a consistent and governed environment in which to build and operate cloud solutions.

Enterprise-scale design means organizing that foundation so it can support multiple teams, applications, environments, and business units without every team inventing its own approach.

Key terms:

* Tenant: The Microsoft Entra ID directory that represents an organization’s identity boundary.
* Subscription: A boundary used to organize Azure resources, manage access, track costs, and apply governance.
* Management group: A container above subscriptions that lets an organization group subscriptions and apply governance across them.
* Workload: An application or business solution and the resources it needs to operate.
* Governance: Rules and controls that help ensure resources meet organizational requirements.
* Shared services: Capabilities used by multiple workloads, such as central networking, DNS, monitoring, or security services.

Legacy equivalent: A combination of enterprise network standards, Active Directory and access controls, shared infrastructure, environment separation, and deployment governance in an on-premises data centre.

2 Why needed

Without a planned foundation, teams may create subscriptions with inconsistent access, networking, security, and resource-management practices. Fixing these differences later can be expensive and disruptive.

A landing zone helps an organization:

* Apply consistent security and governance across subscriptions.
* Separate platform responsibilities from application responsibilities.
* Centralize selected services while allowing workload teams to deploy independently.
* Organize costs and access according to business or technical boundaries.
* Connect cloud workloads to each other and, where needed, to on-premises systems.
* Support growth without redesigning the entire Azure environment every time a team or application is added.

The trade-off is that a highly centralized design can slow teams down. The goal is to standardize essential controls while allowing appropriate freedom within those controls.

4 Features and core concepts

* Azure landing zone concepts

  * Plain meaning: A reusable foundation that establishes how cloud resources are organized, connected, secured, and governed.
  * Why it matters: Workloads start with agreed standards instead of requiring each team to design the entire environment from scratch.

* Management group hierarchy

  * Plain meaning: A tree-shaped structure above subscriptions. Management groups can contain subscriptions or other management groups.
  * Why it matters: Governance can be applied at an appropriate level and inherited by lower levels. For example, an organization can group subscriptions by platform, business unit, or environment.
  * Design consideration: Keep the hierarchy understandable. Excessive nesting makes ownership and policy inheritance harder to reason about.

* Subscription design

  * Plain meaning: Deciding how many subscriptions to create and which workloads or teams belong in each one.
  * Why it matters: Subscriptions help establish boundaries for access, governance, resource organization, and cost reporting.
  * Design consideration: Do not automatically create one subscription per application or put every application in one subscription. Choose boundaries based on isolation, ownership, compliance, operational needs, and scale.

* Hub-spoke networking

  * Plain meaning: A hub virtual network provides central networking capabilities, while separate spoke virtual networks host workloads. Virtual network peering connects the networks where required.
  * Why it matters: Shared connectivity and network controls can be managed centrally while applications remain separated.
  * Trade-off: Central network components can become bottlenecks or create dependencies. Routing, security inspection, and availability must be designed deliberately.

* Azure Virtual WAN

  * Plain meaning: A managed networking service that helps connect distributed sites, branches, and Azure virtual networks through a central networking architecture.
  * Why it matters: It can simplify connectivity for organizations with many locations or networks.
  * Trade-off: It introduces a different networking architecture and associated costs. Choose it based on connectivity requirements, operational scale, and existing network design. Service capabilities and pricing may change (verify).

* Shared services

  * Plain meaning: Common capabilities used by multiple workloads, such as monitoring, DNS, connectivity, or security tooling.
  * Why it matters: Shared services reduce duplication and improve consistency.
  * Trade-off: Central services need clear ownership, capacity planning, and availability arrangements. A shared service failure can affect multiple workloads.

* Policy at scale

  * Plain meaning: Azure Policy evaluates resources against organizational rules and can audit or enforce supported requirements.
  * Why it matters: Organizations can standardize requirements such as permitted configurations and resource locations across multiple subscriptions.
  * Design consideration: Begin by understanding and auditing the intended rules. A poorly tested enforcement policy can block valid deployments or disrupt operations.

* Identity and connectivity subscriptions

  * Plain meaning: A landing-zone design may separate identity-related platform responsibilities and central connectivity resources into dedicated subscriptions.
  * Why it matters: Ownership, permissions, costs, and lifecycle management can be clearer.
  * Design consideration: These are architectural choices, not a universal requirement for every organization. Define which services belong in each subscription and who operates them.

* Platform versus workload teams

  * Plain meaning: Platform teams build and operate shared foundations. Workload teams build and operate particular applications using those foundations.
  * Why it matters: Clear responsibilities prevent gaps in security, networking, monitoring, and operational support.
  * Example: A platform team maintains approved connectivity patterns. An application team deploys its application into an approved workload subscription.

* Cloud Adoption Framework phases

  * Plain meaning: Microsoft's Cloud Adoption Framework provides guidance for planning and managing cloud adoption. Its commonly described methodology includes Strategy, Plan, Ready, Adopt, Govern, Secure, and Manage.
  * Why it matters: A landing zone is part of a broader adoption effort involving business goals, readiness, migration or innovation, governance, security, and ongoing operations.
  * Design consideration: These activities are not necessarily a simple one-time sequence. Governance, security, and management continue throughout adoption. Confirm the current framework terminology and guidance (verify).

5 Core focus

For architecture exams and interviews, focus on explaining the reasoning behind the design rather than merely naming Azure services.

* Explain how management groups, subscriptions, and resource groups differ.
* Describe how governance can be applied across a hierarchy and inherited by lower levels.
* Compare hub-spoke networking with Virtual WAN at a high level.
* Explain why identity, connectivity, and shared services may have separate ownership or subscriptions.
* Show how platform teams enable workload teams without taking over application ownership.
* Connect landing-zone decisions to the Cloud Adoption Framework.
* Discuss trade-offs involving isolation, governance, operational complexity, cost, and team autonomy.

6 Real-world use case

A company is moving several .NET applications from its own data centre to Azure. Different teams own the applications, but the company wants consistent security and connectivity.

A possible design is:

* Use management groups to organize subscriptions and apply common governance.
* Create platform subscriptions for shared responsibilities where separation is useful.
* Use a central connectivity design, such as hub-spoke or Virtual WAN, according to network requirements.
* Place application workloads in subscriptions aligned with ownership, isolation, and governance needs.
* Apply Azure Policy to audit or enforce approved configurations.
* Agree on responsibilities between platform and workload teams.
* Use Cloud Adoption Framework guidance to connect technical implementation with adoption planning and ongoing operations.

Trade-off: Centralization improves consistency but can increase coordination and create shared dependencies. A smaller organization with a few independent workloads may need a simpler design rather than a complex enterprise hierarchy. Avoid adopting a large enterprise pattern solely because it is available.

7 Interview lens

* Q: What is an Azure landing zone?

  * A: It is a governed foundation for deploying and operating Azure workloads.
  * It brings together subscription organization, identity, networking, governance, security, and shared services.

* Q: How do management groups help enterprise-scale design?

  * A: They organize subscriptions into a hierarchy and support governance at higher levels.
  * This reduces the need to configure every subscription independently, provided policy inheritance is designed correctly.

* Q: When would you choose hub-spoke instead of Virtual WAN?

  * A: Hub-spoke can suit an organization that wants a deliberately designed central network and connected workload networks.
  * Virtual WAN can suit broader distributed connectivity needs; the choice depends on topology, operational requirements, capabilities, and cost.

* Q: Why separate platform and workload teams?

  * A: Platform teams provide reusable, governed foundations, while workload teams focus on their applications.
  * Clear ownership supports consistency without requiring the platform team to manage every application detail.

8 Common mistakes

* Treating a landing zone as only a networking solution.
* Creating a complicated management group hierarchy without clear ownership or purpose.
* Assuming every application must have its own subscription.
* Putting unrelated workloads together without evaluating isolation, access, and cost ownership.
* Applying restrictive policies before testing their effect on real deployments.
* Choosing Virtual WAN or hub-spoke without examining actual connectivity needs.
* Centralizing shared services without planning availability, support, and failure impact.
* Confusing platform-team responsibility with ownership of every application.
* Treating the Cloud Adoption Framework as a one-time migration checklist instead of guidance for ongoing adoption, governance, security, and management.

9 Mental model

Think of an Azure landing zone as a managed business campus.

* The management group hierarchy is the campus's organizational structure.
* Subscriptions are independently managed areas with defined ownership and boundaries.
* Networking connects buildings and controls how traffic moves.
* Shared services provide facilities used by multiple teams.
* Azure Policy establishes rules for how the campus must operate.
* Platform teams maintain the common facilities.
* Workload teams run their own businesses inside the agreed rules.
* The Cloud Adoption Framework guides the organization from deciding why to adopt cloud through preparing, adopting, governing, securing, and managing it.

10 Mini lab

Goal: Build a small demonstration of the relationship between management groups, subscriptions, and policy without deploying production infrastructure.

Time: 20–40 minutes.

Tools: An Azure subscription with sufficient permissions to view the relevant hierarchy and create or assign resources. Azure portal access is sufficient. Management-group and policy permissions vary by tenant.

Steps:

* Sign in to the Azure portal.
* Open Management groups and inspect the hierarchy available to your account.
* Identify the tenant root management group and any management groups visible to you. Do not change the existing hierarchy.
* Open Subscriptions and note which subscriptions you can access. Identify the management group relationship where visible.
* Open Azure Policy and inspect available policy definitions or initiatives. A policy initiative is a collection of policy definitions grouped for a related purpose.
* Select a non-disruptive policy suitable for auditing a configuration, if your permissions and subscription allow it. Review its scope and effects before assigning anything.
* If you have permission, create a test resource group in a suitable non-production subscription and inspect which policies apply. Do not create expensive resources.
* If policy assignment permissions are unavailable, use the existing policy assignments and resource compliance views as a read-only exercise.

Expected result:

* You can explain the hierarchy from management groups to subscriptions.
* You can identify where governance is configured and how scope affects resources.
* You understand the difference between inspecting a policy and enforcing one.
* You can describe how a landing zone would organize the platform and workload responsibilities.

Clean-up:

* Delete the test resource group if you created one and no resources need to be retained.
* Remove only policy assignments or other objects that you created for this lab, and only if safe and permitted.
* Do not delete or modify existing management groups, subscriptions, or organization-wide policies.

11 Revision summary

* An Azure landing zone is a governed foundation for deploying workloads.
* Management groups organize subscriptions and enable governance across a hierarchy.
* Subscription boundaries should reflect ownership, isolation, governance, and operational needs.
* Hub-spoke and Virtual WAN are networking approaches with different design and operational trade-offs.
* Shared services improve consistency but create dependencies that need operational planning.
* Azure Policy helps audit or enforce organizational requirements at defined scopes.
* Platform teams operate common foundations; workload teams own their applications.
* The Cloud Adoption Framework connects landing-zone design with broader cloud adoption and ongoing operations.
