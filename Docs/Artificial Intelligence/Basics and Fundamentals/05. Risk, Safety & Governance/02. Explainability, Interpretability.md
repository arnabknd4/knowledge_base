## 1. Learn It (Core Understanding)
- **One-line definition:** Interpretability is how well a human can understand a model's internal mechanics (how it works); explainability is the ability to give understandable reasons for a model's outputs or decisions (why it produced this result), often using extra techniques on top of the model.
- **Plain-English explanation (3-4 lines):**
  Some models are transparent by design: a short decision tree or a simple scoring formula can be read and followed directly. Complex models, such as deep neural networks and LLMs, are "black boxes": their behaviour comes from millions or billions of interacting weights. Explainability techniques try to shed light on such models, for example by showing which inputs mattered most for a specific decision. The two terms are often used interchangeably, but interpretability is usually about understanding the model itself, while explainability is about communicating reasons for outputs to a particular audience.
- **Why it exists / problem it solves:**
  - People affected by AI decisions (loan denial, job rejection) need reasons, and often have legal rights to them
  - Developers need to debug models, detect bias, and spot spurious shortcuts (e.g., a model classifying by image background)
  - Regulators, auditors, and risk teams need evidence that a model behaves sensibly before relying on it
  - Users calibrate trust: they need to know when to rely on AI and when to override it
- **Key terms to know:**
  - **Interpretability:** Degree to which a human can understand how the model works internally
  - **Explainability (XAI):** Providing understandable reasons for a model's outputs, often via added techniques
  - **Black-box model:** Model whose internal logic is too complex to follow directly (deep networks, large ensembles)
  - **White-box / glass-box model:** Inherently understandable model (linear/logistic regression, small decision trees, rule lists)
  - **Intrinsic (ante-hoc) interpretability:** Transparency built into the model design
  - **Post-hoc explainability:** Explanation generated after training, from the outside, for a model that is not transparent
  - **Global explanation:** Describes overall model behaviour (which features matter across all predictions)
  - **Local explanation:** Explains one specific prediction (why this applicant was declined)
  - **Model-agnostic vs model-specific:** Technique works on any model vs tied to one model type
  - **Feature importance:** Ranking of inputs by how much they influence predictions
  - **LIME:** Local explanation by fitting a simple model around one prediction
  - **SHAP:** Assigns each feature a contribution to a prediction (based on Shapley values from game theory); supports local and global views
  - **Partial dependence / ICE plots:** Show how changing one feature changes the prediction on average / for individual cases
  - **Saliency maps / Grad-CAM:** Highlight which image regions (or tokens) influenced a neural network's output
  - **Counterfactual explanation:** "What would need to change to get a different outcome?" (e.g., "approved if income were X higher")
  - **Surrogate model:** Simple interpretable model trained to mimic a complex one
  - **Mechanistic interpretability:** Research field reverse-engineering the internal circuits and features of neural networks
  - **Explanation fidelity (faithfulness):** How accurately an explanation reflects what the model actually did
  - **Reason codes / adverse action notices:** Short statements of the main factors behind a decision, used in credit
  - **Model card / datasheet:** Standard documentation of purpose, data, performance, and limits (supports transparency)
  - **Rationalisation risk:** A plausible-sounding explanation that does not match the model's true reasoning (including LLM self-explanations)
- **How it works (simple steps):**
  1. **Decide the need:** Who needs an explanation (affected person, auditor, developer, regulator) and how high are the stakes?
  2. **Choose the approach:** Use an inherently interpretable model where performance allows; otherwise add post-hoc techniques (SHAP, LIME, counterfactuals).
  3. **Generate explanations:** Global (overall behaviour) for validation and audit; local (single decision) for affected individuals.
  4. **Validate the explanations:** Check fidelity (do they match model behaviour?), stability (similar inputs give similar explanations), and usefulness for the audience.
  5. **Communicate appropriately:** Plain-language reasons for individuals; technical detail for validators and auditors.
  6. **Document and monitor:** Record the approach in model documentation and re-check as data and models change.
- **Where it sits in the AI stack:** Cuts across model and governance layers: a model design choice, an analysis tool set, and a transparency and accountability control.

## 2. Mental Model
- **Analogy:** **A doctor's diagnosis.** A patient wants to know *why* they were told to have surgery. A doctor who says "the algorithm said so" is not good enough. A good doctor points to the scan, the symptoms, and the thresholds that drove the decision, and says what would change the recommendation. A simple rule-of-thumb diagnosis is like a white-box model (you can follow every step). An expert with intuition built over decades is like a deep network (accurate but hard to put into words). Explainability techniques are like asking that expert to walk you through which evidence mattered, with the caveat that their story might be tidier than the real thought process.
- **Diagram / flow:**
```
  INTERPRETABILITY SPECTRUM (typical, not absolute)
  Easy to understand ◄─────────────────────────────────────► Hard to understand
  Rules / small     Linear & logistic   Random forests,     Deep neural networks,
  decision tree     regression          boosted trees       LLMs
  (white box)                                               (black box)
       │                                        │                    │
  Intrinsic interpretability              Post-hoc explanation needed (SHAP, LIME, ...)

  WHICH EXPLANATION?
  One person asks "why me?"   ──► LOCAL (single decision, reason codes, counterfactual)
  Auditor asks "how does it   ──► GLOBAL (feature importance, PDPs, documentation)
  behave overall?"

  CAUTION: Explanation ≠ guarantee. Check fidelity. Explanations can be wrong or misleading.
```
- **The one thing to remember:** Interpretability = understanding how the model works; explainability = giving reasons for its outputs. Use the simplest model that does the job, and treat post-hoc explanations as evidence that must itself be validated.
- **Compare and contrast:**

  | | Interpretability | Explainability |
  |---|---|---|
  | Focus | How the model works internally | Why a given output or decision was produced |
  | Typical source | Model design (transparent by construction) | Added techniques or narrative on top of the model |
  | Audience | Developers, validators | Affected individuals, users, regulators, auditors |
  | Example | Reading the coefficients of a logistic regression | SHAP values showing why one applicant was declined |

  | | Global explanation | Local explanation |
  |---|---|---|
  | Scope | Whole model behaviour | One prediction |
  | Questions answered | "What drives the model overall?" | "Why did this case get this result?" |
  | Techniques | Feature importance, PDP, surrogate models, documentation | LIME, SHAP (per prediction), counterfactuals, reason codes |
  | Main users | Validators, auditors, developers | Affected individuals, case handlers |

  | | Intrinsic (white-box) | Post-hoc (on a black-box) |
  |---|---|---|
  | Explanation faithful to the model? | Yes, the model is the explanation | Approximate; may miss or distort behaviour |
  | Accuracy on complex data | Sometimes lower | Often higher |
  | Audit effort | Lower | Higher (must validate the explainer too) |
  | Best for | High-stakes, regulated decisions on structured data | Complex data (images, text) where simpler models underperform |

  | | Transparency | Explainability |
  |---|---|---|
  | Meaning | Openness about AI use, purpose, data, limits | Reasons for a specific output |
  | Example | Model card; notice that AI is used | "Declined mainly due to high debt-to-income ratio" |

## 3. 🎯 Exam Focus
- **Likely question types:** Definition / Compare (interpretability vs explainability; global vs local; intrinsic vs post-hoc) / Match-technique-to-need / Scenario (regulated decision) / Pick-the-limitation
- **Must-memorize facts:**
  - [ ] **Interpretability** = understanding the model's mechanics; **explainability** = reasons for outputs (terms often used loosely, so read the question context)
  - [ ] Inherently interpretable: **linear/logistic regression, small decision trees, rule lists**; black boxes: **deep neural networks, LLMs, large ensembles**
  - [ ] **Intrinsic (ante-hoc)** vs **post-hoc** explainability
  - [ ] **Global** (overall behaviour) vs **local** (single decision) explanations
  - [ ] Common techniques: **feature importance, partial dependence plots, LIME, SHAP, counterfactuals, saliency maps/Grad-CAM, surrogate models**
  - [ ] **SHAP** = feature contributions based on Shapley values; **LIME** = local simple approximation around one prediction
  - [ ] **Counterfactual** explanations tell a person what would need to change to get a different outcome (actionable)
  - [ ] Key trade-off: **accuracy vs explainability**, but complex models are not always more accurate on structured data
  - [ ] Post-hoc explanations are **approximations** and can be **unstable or misleading**; check **fidelity**
  - [ ] **Attention weights are not reliable explanations**, and **LLM chain-of-thought or self-explanations may not reflect the real internal process**
  - [ ] **NIST's four principles of explainable AI:** Explanation, Meaningful, Explanation Accuracy, Knowledge Limits
  - [ ] **NIST AI RMF** lists "explainable and interpretable" as a trustworthy-AI characteristic
  - [ ] **EU AI Act:** high-risk systems must be transparent enough for deployers to interpret output and use it appropriately (Art. 13) and enable human oversight (Art. 14); affected persons can request a clear and meaningful explanation of the AI system's role in certain high-risk decisions (Art. 86)
  - [ ] **GDPR:** rules on solely automated decisions with legal or similarly significant effects (Art. 22); individuals are entitled to meaningful information about the logic involved (Arts. 13-15)
  - [ ] **US credit:** lenders must give specific reasons for adverse actions (adverse action notices), which applies to complex models too
  - [ ] Explanations must be **appropriate to the audience** (technical vs lay)
  - [ ] Explainability supports **bias detection, debugging, trust calibration, contestability, and accountability**
- **Keywords that signal this topic in a question:** "black box," "why was this decision made," "right to explanation," "reason codes," "SHAP," "LIME," "feature importance," "counterfactual," "transparent model," "auditability," "contest the decision"
- **Commonly confused with:**
  - Interpretability vs explainability (mechanics vs reasons)
  - Explainability vs transparency (reasons for outputs vs openness about system and use)
  - Global vs local explanations
  - Feature importance vs causation (importance shows association in the model, not real-world cause)
  - Explanation fidelity vs plausibility (an explanation can sound convincing and still be wrong)
  - Explainability vs accuracy or fairness (explaining a decision does not make it correct or fair)
  - Traceability/logging (recording what happened) vs explainability (why)
- **Elimination tips (how to rule out wrong options):**
  - Reject "explainability tools prove the model is fair or correct." They describe behaviour; they do not validate it.
  - Reject "feature importance shows what *causes* the outcome in the real world."
  - Reject "the most complex model is always the most accurate," especially for structured data.
  - Reject "a plausible explanation is a faithful one."
  - Reject "an LLM's own explanation of its answer is reliable evidence of how it reached it."
  - For **high-stakes, regulated decisions on structured data** → prefer an inherently interpretable model or well-validated explanations plus documentation.
  - For **an individual asking why they were declined** → local explanation with meaningful, actionable reasons and a route to human review.
  - For **model validation or audit** → global explanations, documentation, testing, and validation of the explainer itself.
  - For **"what can the customer do differently?"** → counterfactual explanation.
- **Frameworks / standards link:**
  - **EU AI Act:** Transparency and provision of information to deployers (Art. 13), human oversight enabling people to correctly interpret outputs (Art. 14), technical documentation, and the right to explanation for affected persons in specified high-risk decisions (Art. 86). Transparency duties for chatbots and AI-generated content are related but separate. Obligations apply in phases, so verify current dates.
  - **NIST AI RMF:** "Explainable and interpretable" is one of the trustworthy characteristics, and it links to "accountable and transparent." NIST's separate work on explainable AI defines the four principles (Explanation, Meaningful, Explanation Accuracy, Knowledge Limits).
  - **ISO/IEC 42001:** Requires documentation and transparency appropriate to interested parties and verification and validation across the lifecycle.
  - **GDPR and financial regulation:** Automated decision-making rules and meaningful information about logic; credit adverse-action requirements; model risk management expectations (e.g., SR 11-7 in the US) for understanding and validating models.
  - **Governance angle:** Explainability should be proportionate to risk: the higher the impact on people, the stronger the expectation of meaningful, tested explanations and human review.

## 4. 🛠️ Real-World Use Cases
| # | Scenario | Industry | How this topic applies | Outcome / risk |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Loan approval and decline reasons | Banking | Reason codes or SHAP-based local explanations; counterfactuals for applicants | Meets notice duties and supports appeals; misleading explanations create legal and trust risk |
| 2 | Diagnostic imaging support | Healthcare | Saliency/Grad-CAM heatmaps show image regions that drove the prediction | Helps clinicians check plausibility; heatmaps can be unreliable or over-trusted |
| 3 | Fraud alert investigation | Payments | Feature contributions tell analysts why a transaction was flagged | Faster, better triage; explanations may expose detection logic to fraudsters if shared externally |
| 4 | Hiring or promotion screening | HR | Explanations used to check for proxies and bias; reasons provided to candidates where required | Detects hidden discrimination; risk if explanations hide proxy variables |
| 5 | Model debugging | Data science | Global importance reveals a spurious shortcut (e.g., scan hospital ID predicts outcome) | Prevents deployment of a model that would fail in the real world |
| 6 | LLM assistant giving reasoning for its answer | Cross-industry | Model's stated reasoning shown to users | May be a post-hoc rationalisation; needs verification against sources |

- **Consulting / assurance lens:**
  - Ask the client: "For each AI-supported decision, who needs an explanation, what form does it take, and how do you know the explanation is accurate?"
  - Look for proportionality: stronger explanation and oversight for decisions with legal or significant effects on people.
- **Evidence or controls you would look for:**
  - Documented rationale for model choice (why this complexity, compared with an interpretable baseline)
  - Explainability approach per use case, matched to audience and stakes
  - Validation of explanations (fidelity, stability, sensitivity checks), not just generation
  - Model cards and technical documentation describing behaviour, limits, and known failure modes
  - Customer-facing reason statements and a route to contest and request human review
  - Analyst and reviewer training on interpreting outputs and explanations (and their limits)
  - Logging and traceability of inputs, outputs, and versions to support after-the-fact review
  - Independent model validation covering explainability (per model risk management policy)

## 5. Q&A Bank

### Quick recall
- **Q:** What is the difference between interpretability and explainability?
  **A:** Interpretability is understanding how a model works internally; explainability is giving understandable reasons for its outputs or decisions. The terms are often used interchangeably.

- **Q:** Name two inherently interpretable models.
  **A:** Linear or logistic regression and small decision trees (also rule lists).

- **Q:** What is the difference between a global and a local explanation?
  **A:** Global describes the model's overall behaviour; local explains a single prediction.

### Conceptual
- **Q:** What do LIME and SHAP do?
  **A:** Both are model-agnostic, post-hoc techniques for explaining individual predictions. LIME fits a simple local model around the prediction; SHAP assigns each feature a contribution score based on Shapley values and can be aggregated into global views.

- **Q:** What is a counterfactual explanation and why is it useful?
  **A:** It states what would need to change to get a different outcome (e.g., "approved if annual income were higher by X"). It is actionable for the affected person and often easier to understand than a list of weights.

- **Q:** Is there always a trade-off between accuracy and explainability?
  **A:** Often, but not always. On structured or tabular data, simple interpretable models can perform about as well as complex ones, so the trade-off should be tested rather than assumed.

- **Q:** Why can post-hoc explanations be misleading?
  **A:** They approximate the model from the outside, so they can be unfaithful, unstable (small input changes giving very different explanations), or simply plausible stories. They must be validated for fidelity.

- **Q:** Do feature importance scores show causes?
  **A:** No. They show how much the model relies on a feature, which reflects patterns in the data, including correlations and proxies, not necessarily real-world causation.

- **Q:** Can an LLM explain its own reasoning reliably?
  **A:** Not necessarily. Its stated reasoning may be a plausible rationalisation rather than a faithful account of its internal computation, so important outputs should be verified against sources.

### Scenario-based
- **Q:** A bank uses a complex model to decline a loan. The applicant asks why. What should the bank provide?
  **A:** Specific, meaningful reasons drawn from a validated local explanation (reason codes or equivalent), ideally with what could change the outcome, plus a route to human review and to contest the decision, consistent with applicable law (e.g., adverse action requirements, GDPR automated-decision rules).

- **Q:** Model validation finds that a pneumonia classifier relies heavily on the hospital's marker in the X-ray corner. What does this show and what should be done?
  **A:** The model learned a spurious shortcut, which explainability analysis exposed. Do not deploy; fix the data (remove the artefact, diversify sources), retrain, and validate on independent data from other sites.

- **Q:** A vendor says its model is "fully explainable" because it provides a heat map. How should an assurance professional respond?
  **A:** Ask how explanation fidelity was tested, whether explanations are stable, who the audience is, and whether the explanation is meaningful for decisions affecting people. A heat map alone is not evidence of sound reasoning.

- **Q:** A team must choose between a slightly more accurate black-box model and an interpretable model for automated insurance pricing decisions. What should guide the choice?
  **A:** The stakes and regulatory expectations. Compare the real accuracy gain, the ability to explain and contest decisions, bias detection needs, and validation effort. If the gain is small, the interpretable model is usually the safer choice; if a black box is justified, add validated explanations, documentation, and human oversight.

### Interview-style
- **Q:** "Explain explainability to a non-technical executive."
  **A:** "If our AI declines a customer, approves a claim, or flags a transaction, we need to be able to say why, in terms a person can understand and challenge. Some models are simple enough to read like a recipe; the most powerful ones behave more like a brilliant expert who can't fully articulate their intuition. So we either choose simpler models for high-stakes decisions or add tools that show which factors mattered, and then test that those explanations are accurate. It matters for customer trust, for spotting hidden bias and errors, and increasingly for the law."

## 6. ✅ Dos and ❌ Don'ts

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Scale explainability to the stakes and the audience | Treat one explanation format as right for everyone |
| Compare against a simple interpretable baseline before choosing a black box | Assume the most complex model is necessary |
| Provide meaningful, specific reasons for decisions affecting people | Give vague or boilerplate reasons |
| Validate explanations (fidelity, stability, usefulness) | Trust an explanation just because it looks convincing |
| Use global explanations for validation and audit, local ones for individuals | Use only one type and assume it covers both needs |
| Offer a route to contest decisions and get human review | Present AI explanations as final |
| Document model behaviour, limits, and failure modes (model cards) | Rely on undocumented model behaviour |
| Train reviewers to interpret outputs and recognise over-trust | Assume staff will know when to challenge the AI |
| Verify LLM outputs against sources, not just its stated reasoning | Accept an LLM's self-explanation as proof |
| Re-check explanations after retraining or drift | Assume explanations stay valid when the model or data changes |

- **Common mistakes / misconceptions:**
  1. "Explainability and interpretability are identical." (Related but distinct; be precise.)
  2. "Feature importance equals causation." (It reflects the model's reliance, not real-world causes.)
  3. "A heat map or SHAP chart proves the model is right or fair." (It describes behaviour; it does not validate it.)
  4. "Explainability always costs accuracy." (Not always, especially on tabular data.)
  5. "Explainability is only for regulators." (Also for debugging, trust, and user decisions.)
  6. "Transparency and explainability are the same." (Openness about the system vs reasons for an output.)
  7. "The LLM told me how it reasoned, so it's auditable." (Stated reasoning may not be faithful.)
- **Red flags in practice:**
  - Black-box model used for high-impact decisions with no explanation route
  - Explanations never tested for accuracy or stability
  - Decision reasons that cannot be given to affected individuals
  - Vendor claims of "explainable AI" with no methodology
  - Reviewers who approve AI outputs without understanding them
  - No documentation of why a complex model was chosen over a simpler one

## 7. Revision Snapshot
- **30-second summary:**
  Interpretability is understanding how a model works; explainability is giving understandable reasons for its outputs. Simple models (logistic regression, small trees) are interpretable by design; complex ones (deep networks, LLMs) need post-hoc tools such as SHAP, LIME, counterfactuals, and saliency maps, offering local (one decision) and global (overall behaviour) views. Post-hoc explanations are approximations that must be validated for fidelity, and feature importance is not causation. Explainability supports trust, debugging, bias detection, contestability, and legal duties (EU AI Act transparency and oversight, GDPR automated-decision rules, credit adverse-action reasons, NIST's "explainable and interpretable" characteristic). Match the depth of explanation to the stakes, compare against interpretable baselines, and keep meaningful human oversight.
- **3 bullets to recall on exam day:**
  1. Interpretability = how it works; explainability = why this output; global vs local; intrinsic vs post-hoc
  2. Tools: SHAP, LIME, counterfactuals, feature importance, saliency maps; all need fidelity checks and none prove fairness or causation
  3. Match to stakes: interpretable model or validated explanations for high-impact decisions, with reasons, appeal route, and human review
