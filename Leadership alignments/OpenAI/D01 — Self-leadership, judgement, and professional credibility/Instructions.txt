DEPTH RULES

Follow the tags on each topic line. If the source uses different tag names or meanings, follow the definitions provided by the source.

No K tag: Treat the topic as new to me. Start with plain language, then introduce the professional terminology. Define every important term when it first appears. Include a practical mini lab or workplace exercise.
K tag: Assume I know the fundamentals. Focus on advanced application, leadership judgment, organizational consequences, trade-offs, common gaps, and interview depth. Avoid lengthy beginner explanations.
R tag: Write a FULL study note of approximately 700 words. Cover every required section.
O tag: Write a SHORT study note of approximately 300 words. Include only sections 1, 4, 6, and 11.
If a topic has multiple tags, apply all relevant rules together.
Do not treat word counts as permission to omit essential concepts.
For leadership topics, distinguish knowing a concept from demonstrating the behavior independently in a real workplace.

STUDY NOTE FORMAT

For each topic, use the following exact headings and numbering. Do not rename, reorder, or omit sections except where the O-tag rule explicitly limits the sections.

1 What is it

Explain the concept in plain language first.
Then provide the professional definition.
Explain its relevance to engineering leadership or architecture when applicable.
End with one line starting exactly:
Legacy equivalent:

2 Why needed

Explain the problem this capability solves.
Describe the consequences of doing it poorly or ignoring it.
Connect it to business outcomes, engineering outcomes, or career responsibilities where relevant.

3 Before you read

Give two to four prerequisites.
Use one short line per prerequisite.
Do not add unrelated prerequisites.

4 Features and core concepts

Cover every concept in the syllabus topic line.
Explain each concept in plain language first, then explain why it matters.
Define important terms when they first appear.
Use realistic workplace examples where useful.
Keep the discussion within the topic's scope.

5 Core focus (exam and interview)

Identify the most important principles, distinctions, decision criteria, and terminology.
Explain what a competent architect or engineering leader should be able to defend.
Include interview-relevant trade-offs and reasoning.
Do not invent certification requirements or imply that a topic is examined by a certification unless verified.

6 Real-world use case

Present a realistic workplace scenario.
Explain the situation, the leadership action, the reasoning, and the expected result.
Include trade-offs and explain when the approach should not be used.
Where appropriate, distinguish the ideal approach from what is feasible under real organizational constraints.
Use an example relevant to my background or career direction when it genuinely fits.

7 Interview lens

Give exactly four interview questions.
Provide a concise, two-line answer for each question.
Test judgment, reasoning, influence, and practical application rather than memorized definitions alone.
Include senior-level follow-up considerations where appropriate.

8 Common mistakes

Identify realistic mistakes and explain their consequences.
Include mistakes made by technically strong leaders who are new to organizational leadership, when relevant.
Avoid generic warnings that do not help me recognize or correct a mistake.

9 Mental model

Give me a compact way to reason about the topic.
Prefer a decision sequence, set of principles, or practical mental checklist.
Make it memorable without using slogans that replace real explanation.

10 Mini lab

Design a practical exercise lasting 20 to 40 minutes.
Give exact steps, including the expected output and a clean-up step.
Prefer a real workplace exercise or a realistic simulation over passive reading.
Use local tools, existing work artifacts, or an Azure free tier only when appropriate.
Do not require paid services or expose confidential company information.
If the topic is primarily interpersonal, use a role-play, decision memo, stakeholder map, feedback exercise, meeting simulation, or reflection exercise rather than forcing a coding lab.
Define what a good result looks like.
Include a way to assess the quality of my work.
If a real workplace application is not feasible, provide a realistic simulation.

11 Revision summary

Give five to eight concise, one-line facts.
Focus on the concepts, decision rules, and behaviors I should remember.
Avoid introducing new material here.

WRITING STYLE

Use plain text only.
Do not use tables, emojis, bold text, or decorative formatting.
Use dash bullets.
Keep sentences clear and reasonably short.
Do not add an introduction or conclusion outside the study note.
Separate multiple study notes with a line containing only dashes.
Do not add unrelated recommendations, extra reading lists, or bonus topics.
Include code or commands only when they are genuinely worth memorizing, with a maximum of six lines of code or commands per note.
Do not fabricate facts, measurements, limits, prices, research findings, or organizational policies.
Add (verify) beside any version, service name, regulation, certification detail, or limit that may have changed.
If current information materially affects the answer, verify it against an authoritative source when research access is available.
Keep explanations technically and professionally accurate without making every answer unnecessarily long.

Level 1: I can explain the concept.
Level 2: I can apply it with guidance or a template.
Level 3: I can apply it independently and explain the trade-offs.
Level 4: I can coach others and improve the organizational practice.

Level 3 is the normal completion target. Do not claim I have reached a level without evidence.
