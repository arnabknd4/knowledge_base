1 What is it

- In plain language: noticing what is going on inside you at work (what sets you off, how stress shows up, what you are assuming) and choosing your response instead of reacting automatically.
- Professional definition: self-awareness is accurate insight into your own triggers, assumptions, and behavior patterns. Emotional regulation is managing your reactions so your actions match your intent and the situation.
- Relevance: as a Tech Lead you set the tone in design reviews, incidents, and estimate negotiations. Architects work through influence, so a defensive or reactive reaction costs you credibility.
- Syllabus scope: triggers; stress signals; assumptions; defensiveness; emotional self-management; reflective practice; separating intent from impact; receiving difficult feedback. Tags: P1 R W.

Legacy equivalent: Debugging your own behavior, using logs (reflection notes) and breakpoints (a pause) instead of reacting to the first symptom.

2 Why needed

- Problem solved: strong engineers often respond to challenge with a fast technical rebuttal. That ends the discussion and hides the real concern.
- Poor regulation consequences: people stop bringing you bad news, reviews become performances, and peers route around you.
- Business effect: unreported risks surface late, as production incidents or missed commitments.
- Career effect: moving to Solution or Enterprise Architect means more disagreement with senior people you cannot overrule. Composure under challenge is a core signal of readiness.
- Evidence note: the syllabus counts only demonstrated behavior. Understanding this topic is level 1.

3 Before you read

- Recall two recent work moments where you felt irritated or defensive.
- Have one trusted colleague in mind who can give honest feedback.
- Know your recent calendar so you can spot pressure points.

4 Features and core concepts

- Triggers: situations that reliably provoke a strong reaction, such as being second-guessed, scope changes after sign-off, or seeing a design you built get criticized. Name them in advance. A named trigger is easier to manage.
- Stress signals: body, thought, and behavior cues that you are overloaded. Examples are clipped replies, skipping reviews, rereading messages angrily, or taking over tasks. Learn your early signals, not only your late ones.
- Assumptions: beliefs you treat as fact without checking. For example, "the product owner ignores technical debt" may really be "nobody has shown her the cost." Label each belief as fact or assumption, then test it.
- Defensiveness: protecting yourself instead of examining the point. Signs include immediate counterarguments, explaining history, or attacking the messenger. It is a signal to pause, not a character flaw.
- Emotional self-management: practical tools to respond rather than react. Pause before replying, breathe, write the angry draft and do not send it, ask a clarifying question first, or ask for time to think.
- Reflective practice: regularly reviewing what happened, what you felt, what you did, and what you would change. A short written log works better than vague thinking.
- Separating intent from impact: your intent is what you meant. Impact is how it landed. Dismissing a junior developer's idea "to save time" can still shut them down. Own the impact without defending the intent.
- Receiving difficult feedback: listen fully, paraphrase it back, ask for a specific example, thank the person, and decide later what to act on. You do not have to agree on the spot.

5 Core focus (exam and interview)

- Distinction: self-awareness is noticing. Regulation is choosing. Reflection is learning from both.
- Principle: you cannot control the trigger, only the gap between trigger and response.
- Defend this: intent matters for understanding yourself, impact matters for leading others.
- Defend this: feedback is data. You may reject it after thought, but you cannot reject it without listening.
- Interview trade-off: composure is not suppression. Hiding frustration entirely can mislead people, so a good answer shows calm, honest expression.
- Do not claim a formal standard exists. The syllabus presents this as practical leadership practice, not a certified framework.

6 Real-world use case

- Situation: you propose migrating a legacy .NET Framework application to current .NET on Azure. A senior developer says publicly, "This is resume-driven design."
- Reaction without regulation: you list your credentials and the migration benefits, and the room turns defensive.
- Leadership action: notice the heat in your chest, pause, and ask, "What risk worries you most?" He says the team lost two sprints to a failed rewrite last year.
- Reasoning: his concern is real, and your assumption that he opposed change was wrong.
- Result: you propose a strangler-style first slice with a rollback plan. He becomes a reviewer instead of an opponent.
- Trade-off: pausing costs a minute and can look slow in urgent incidents. In a live outage, use a short pre-agreed script and reflect afterward.
- Feasibility: if the other person is hostile or acts in bad faith, regulation does not mean tolerating abuse. Address behavior privately or escalate.

7 Interview lens

- Q1: Tell me about a time you got defensive at work.
  A: Describe a real instance honestly, the trigger, what you did, and the impact.
  A: Then show what you changed, with evidence such as a later calmer review.
- Q2: How do you handle a senior stakeholder publicly criticizing your design?
  A: I pause, ask what concern sits behind it, and restate it before answering.
  A: If the issue is real I adjust. If not, I offer evidence privately later.
- Q3: How do you separate intent from impact?
  A: I check how my words landed by asking, then own the effect without justifying my intent.
  A: I follow up with a change in how I communicate.
- Q4: How do you know you are stressed, and what do you do?
  A: I watch for my early signals, such as terse messages and skipped reviews.
  A: I reduce load, take a break, and delay irreversible decisions.

8 Common mistakes

- Winning the argument: responding with facts to a concern about feelings or risk. Result: the concern goes underground.
- Treating assumptions as facts: you decide a stakeholder is obstructive without asking. Result: you create the conflict you feared.
- Apologizing for intent only: "I didn't mean it that way" without addressing impact. Result: trust does not recover.
- Performing calm: staying silent while visibly tense. Result: people read the mismatch and trust you less.
- Collecting feedback and ignoring it: asking, then acting offended or doing nothing. Result: people stop giving it.
- Confusing self-awareness with self-criticism: endless guilt does not improve behavior.

9 Mental model

- Notice: what am I feeling, and what triggered it?
- Name: is this a fact or an assumption?
- Pause: what is the smallest delay that lets me respond well?
- Ask: what is the other person's concern?
- Respond: what action serves the outcome?
- Review: what would I repeat or change?

10 Mini lab

- Duration: 30 minutes, plus two short follow-ups.
- Step 1 (5 min): list five recent work moments where you felt defensive, annoyed, or anxious. Use no names.
- Step 2 (10 min): for each, write the trigger, your stress signal, the assumption you made, what you did, and the likely impact on others.
- Step 3 (5 min): pick one trigger that repeats. Write a one-line pause script, such as "Help me understand the concern."
- Step 4 (5 min): rehearse aloud a response to this feedback: "Your reviews feel dismissive." Paraphrase first, then ask for an example.
- Step 5 (5 min): ask a trusted colleague for one specific example where your reaction affected the team. Record it.
- Expected output: a one-page reflection log and a written pause script.
- Good result: each entry separates intent from impact, labels at least one assumption, and shows one behavior you will change.
- Assessment: check your log against the syllabus. Does it cover triggers, signals, assumptions, defensiveness, and a change plan? Then review at 3, 10, and 30 days to see if behavior changed.
- Clean-up: redact names and company details, and store the log privately.

11 Revision summary

- Self-awareness is noticing your triggers, stress signals, and assumptions.
- Defensiveness is a signal to pause and ask, not to argue.
- Regulation means choosing a response, not hiding emotion.
- Intent explains you. Impact is what you must own.
- Receive feedback by listening, paraphrasing, and asking for examples before deciding.
- Reflective practice needs a written log and repeated review.
- Level 3 means independent application with evidence, not understanding alone.
