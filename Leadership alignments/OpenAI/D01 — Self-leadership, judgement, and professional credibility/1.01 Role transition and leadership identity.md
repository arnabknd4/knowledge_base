1.01 Role transition and leadership identity
Source tags: P1 (lead yourself and delivery), R (required), W (workplace practice). No K tag, so this is treated as new material. Target: level 3 with evidence.

1 What is it
- Plain language: this is about how your job changes as you move from writing software to being responsible for what teams and organizations achieve. The skills that made you a strong engineer are necessary but no longer enough.
- Professional definition: role transition is the deliberate shift in expected outcomes, decision scope, and accountability between roles. Leadership identity is a conscious, stated sense of how you lead, based on principles rather than title.
- Relevance: as a Tech Lead you answer for team delivery and technical quality. As an architect you answer for decisions that span teams, systems, and business outcomes, often without managing anyone.
- Legacy equivalent: the traditional promotion of the best developer into a lead role with no change in how they were measured or coached.

2 Why needed
- Many strong engineers keep working at their old level after promotion. They code the hardest parts themselves and become a bottleneck.
- Consequences: the team does not grow, decisions wait on you, stakeholders see an engineer rather than a leader, and you burn out.
- Business impact: architecture roles are judged on outcomes such as risk reduced, delivery enabled, and cost controlled, not on personal output.
- Career impact: interviews for solution and enterprise architect roles test whether you have already operated at that scope.

3 Before you read
- Your current role description and what your manager expects of you.
- A recent project you led, including who made the key decisions.
- Basic familiarity with the other topics in this syllabus.

4 Features and core concepts
Roles to distinguish (the syllabus names these roles but does not define each one's scope; the descriptions below are common working interpretations, not a formal standard):
- Individual contributor: personal output and technical quality.
- Tech lead: a team's technical direction and delivery. You usually have influence but not formal authority over people.
- Engineering manager: people, staffing, performance, and delivery capacity. Reporting lines and budgets sit here.
- Technical architect: technical decisions for a system. Success is a sound, adopted design.
- Solution architect: fitting a solution to a business problem across systems, vendors, and constraints.
- Enterprise architect: alignment of capabilities, standards, and investments across the organization. Influence is long-range and mostly indirect.

Authority vs accountability:
- Authority is the right to decide or direct. Accountability is being answerable for the result.
- Architects often hold accountability without authority. A design you propose may be funded or rejected by others.
- Plain consequence: you must influence, document, and escalate rather than instruct.

Outcomes expected at each level:
- Move from "my code works" to "the team delivers" to "the system suits the business" to "the portfolio supports strategy".
- Ask at each level: what result am I answerable for, and who judges it?

Avoiding title-driven behavior:
- Title-driven behavior means acting from rank: demanding deference, deciding alone to look decisive, or avoiding work seen as beneath the title.
- It damages trust and hides your real contribution.

Personal leadership principles:
- A short written set of rules you will follow under pressure, for example "surface bad news early" or "decide with evidence, record why".
- They only count if others can observe them in your behavior.

5 Core focus (exam and interview)
- Be able to state what changes in each role transition: scope, outcomes, and how you are measured.
- Defend the difference between authority and accountability with a real example.
- Explain how you lead without formal authority: evidence, relationships, clear options, escalation.
- Know that title-driven behavior is a failure mode, and name your own risks.
- Principles must be specific and evidenced. Slogans fail interviews.
- No certification requirement is claimed for this topic.

6 Real-world use case
- Situation: you are Tech Lead on a legacy ASP.NET MVC and SQL Server application. The business wants to move to Azure. You are asked to shape the approach but do not own the budget or the other teams.
- Old behavior: you design the target system alone and present it as final.
- Leadership action: you write a short options memo with a do-nothing option, name the decision owner, interview the operations and security leads about their constraints, and ask your manager which decisions are yours.
- Reasoning: the outcome needed is an approved, adopted plan, not a correct diagram.
- Expected result: faster agreement, clearer ownership, and a recorded decision.
- Trade-offs: this takes more time up front. Do not use it for small reversible choices inside your own team, where deciding quickly is better.
- Feasible vs ideal: if stakeholders are unavailable, document assumptions and get a quick review from one peer.

7 Interview lens
- Q: What changes when a tech lead becomes an architect?
  A: The outcome shifts from team delivery to decisions that hold across teams and business needs. You influence more and build less.
- Q: How do you lead when you have accountability but no authority?
  A: I build evidence, align stakeholders on criteria, and make options and risks explicit. I escalate with a recommendation when persuasion stalls.
- Q: Describe a time you stopped acting like an individual contributor.
  A: Give a real story of delegating a design you could have done yourself. State the result and what you learned.
- Q: What are your leadership principles?
  A: Name three, each with a behavior and one example. Admit one principle you broke and what you changed.
- Senior follow-up: how do you know your principles work? Cite feedback or outcomes, not belief.

8 Common mistakes
- Staying the best coder on the team. It creates a bottleneck and starves others of growth.
- Treating a recommendation as an approved decision. The work stalls when the real owner objects.
- Owning outcomes yet withholding decision rights from your team, or taking decision rights without accountability.
- Copying a manager's style instead of choosing principles.
- Writing generic principles such as "be a good listener" with no observable behavior.
- Using the title to end a debate instead of resolving it.

9 Mental model
Ask five questions on any new responsibility:
- What outcome am I answerable for?
- Who actually decides?
- What authority do I have, and what must I earn through influence?
- Which of my old habits does this situation tempt me to repeat?
- Which of my written principles applies here?

10 Mini lab
Time: 35 minutes. Real workplace exercise using only your own notes.
- Step 1 (10 min): list the last five significant decisions you were involved in. For each, mark whether you decided, recommended, or were informed.
- Step 2 (10 min): for each, write who was accountable for the outcome and whether you had authority to act. Note any gaps.
- Step 3 (10 min): draft three personal leadership principles. For each, write one observable behavior and one situation where it would be hard to follow.
- Step 4 (5 min): choose one habit from your IC or tech-lead days that you will change this month.
- Output: a one-page note with the decision table in dash bullets, the three principles, and the habit.
- Clean-up: remove names, customer details, and commercial terms. Store the note privately.
- Good result: the gaps between authority and accountability are specific, and each principle names a behavior another person could observe.
- Assessment: show it to a trusted peer and ask whether your behavior matches the principles. Score yourself 0 to 4 using the syllabus standard. Level 3 needs evidence from at least two real situations. Review at 3, 10, and 30 days.

11 Revision summary
- Each role has different outcomes. Know what you are answerable for.
- Authority is the right to decide. Accountability is answerability for results.
- Architects often hold accountability with limited authority, so influence and escalation are core skills.
- Title-driven behavior erodes trust.
- A recommendation is not an approved decision.
- Principles must be written and observable in behavior.
- Understanding the topic is level 1. Independent application with evidence is level 3.
